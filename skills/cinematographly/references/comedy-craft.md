# Comedy craft — what the evidence supports about being funny for a brand

**Why this file exists.** A client asked for a mix of **serious and humorous** executions for the
same product, at volume, from the same team. The skill had nothing: "punchline" returned zero hits
in the whole tree and humour appeared only as one row in a list of hook types. Comedy was being
treated as a tone setting on a dropdown rather than a craft with its own failure modes — and it has
the most counter-intuitive evidence base of anything in this skill. Nearly every widely-held belief
about funny advertising is either unmeasured or measured and false.

Every claim carries a tier (`research-quality.md` §2). Researched 2026-09-07.

---

## 1. The single most important finding on this page

⛔ **Funniness is not the metric. The absence of a negative reaction is.**

> **[PRIMARY · Warren, Percival Carter & McGraw, "Being funny is not enough: the influence of
> perceived humor and negative emotional reactions on brand attitudes", *International Journal of
> Advertising* 38(7), 2019, pp. 1025–1045 (doi 10.1080/02650487.2019.1620090) · fetched 2026-09-07]** Four studies. The zero-order correlation between how funny an ad is and
> brand attitude is **non-significant in all four**: r = .21 (N=75, 60 print ads), .26 (36 student-made ads rated by 106 students),
> .28 (N=868, 38 Super Bowl 2018 TV ads), −.05 (N=308). Adding *negative reactions* to the model
> moves R² from **4% → 80%**, 7% → 40%, 26% → 35%, 3.5% → 23.4%. Negative reactions and perceived
> humour are **orthogonal**, not opposites (r = −.24 to +.22, all n.s.). The authors: *"Humorous
> advertisements can help or hurt brand attitudes, but the direction… depends primarily on whether
> the ad evokes negative reactions rather than on whether it is funny."*

**This rewrites the review.** A comedy review that asks *"is this funny?"* is measuring a variable
with no measured relationship to the outcome — and asking a question that a small panel is bad at
answering anyway (§4). The question that predicts the outcome is:

> **"Does any part of this produce cringe, irritation, disgust, or embarrassment?"**

That question a panel of three can answer reliably, in one pass, without needing to laugh. Put it on
the review card verbatim. It is also the one comedy question a client can answer for themselves,
which makes approval faster rather than slower.

## 2. What humour buys, and what it costs

> **[PRIMARY · Eisend, "A meta-analysis of humor in advertising", *Journal of the Academy of
> Marketing Science* 37(2), 2009, Table 3 · 43 independent studies, 369 effect sizes, 1960–2006 ·
> fetched 2026-09-07]**

| Outcome | k | mean r | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | 29 | **+0.416** | The biggest effect in the table |
| **Attitude toward the ad** | 87 | **+0.374** | Where the attention goes |
| Attitude toward the brand | 49 | +0.189 | Half the Aad effect |
| Purchase intention | 46 | +0.192 | |
| **Credibility** | 13 | **−0.130** | Small, real, CI excludes zero |
| Recall — ad | 16 | +0.121 | **n.s.** |
| **Recall — brand** | 22 | **+0.071** | **n.s.**, CI [−.050, .190] |
| Recognition — ad / brand | 5 / 17 | +0.224 / +0.161 | **n.s.** / **n.s.** |
| Comprehension | 29 | +0.036 | flat |
| Purchase *behaviour* | 4 | +0.008 | uninformative, k=4 |

**The correct one-sentence summary, which contradicts the folklore:** *humour reliably buys attention
and liking; it does not buy memory for anything, including the ad itself; and it costs a little
credibility.* The decay chain is the thing to hold — **.42 attention → .37 ad-liking → .19
brand-attitude**. Two thirds of the effect is gone before it reaches the brand.

⚠ **Discount all of it for live work.** Eisend's own moderator: **real vs fictitious ads,
coefficient −0.463, p = .001** — effects are substantially smaller for real ads than for the
lab-built stimuli most of this literature uses. Also **student samples +0.354, p < .001**: most of
these participants are undergraduates. Two moderators that go *your* way: **broadcast vs print,
+0.329, p = .016** (humour works better in moving image), and perceived humour intensity → brand
attitude is **linear (b = 0.603, SE = 0.098, p < .001, R² = .526)** with a non-significant quadratic
term — **there is no measured "too funny" ceiling** on brand attitude.

## 3. The rule that decides whether to be funny at all

⛔ **For a high-involvement functional product, the joke must be ABOUT the product's world, or it
does nothing measurable.**

Eisend's moderator model on the humour→brand-attitude correlations (n = 41, **R² = 0.630**) uses the
Weinberger product-colour matrix:

| Cell | Examples | Related humour | Unrelated humour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **White** — high involvement, functional | appliances, insurance, cars, **mattresses** | **β = 0.121, p = .039** | — *not estimated* |
| Blue — low involvement, functional | detergent, batteries | n.s. | n.s. |
| Red — high involvement, hedonic | fashion, sports cars | — | **β = 0.359, p = .004** |
| Yellow — low involvement, hedonic | snacks, beer | **β = 0.377, p < .001** | — |

**A mattress is a white good.** Related humour works and is the *smallest working effect in the
table* — about a third of the yellow-goods effect. ⚠ **White × unrelated was never estimated.**
Eisend's table carries a dash in that cell, not a null, and an earlier draft of this file mis-read it
as "n.s." The honest statement is therefore: *there is no evidence that unrelated humour helps a
white good, and none that it does not* — while the credibility cost of **−0.130** is paid either
way. That supports "do not spend on it". It does not support "it does nothing".

A second literature points the same way — ⚠ **in B2B, not consumer**, which is a scope gap and not a
second measurement of the same thing: **[PRIMARY · Swani, Gulas & Dinsmore, "To humor or not humor
buyers? Evaluating the effective use of humor in B2B advertisements", *Journal of Business Research*
200, 2025 · 4 experiments, total n = 305 · design and direction verified, effect sizes not obtained ·
2026-09-07]** finds humour raises ad- then brand-
attitude by serial mediation, and **fails when the humour is product-unrelated**, when an established
vendor relationship exists, and when the buyer is under time pressure. The last two are B2B purchase
constructs; only the relatedness finding transfers, and it transfers as corroboration of direction,
not as a second effect size.

**The operational line, and it is sharp:** a funny video whose joke is about *sleep, beds, backs,
mornings, partners, the 3am ceiling* is supported by the evidence. A funny video that is merely
funny — a bit, a trending format, a sketch that could sell anything — has **no measured brand
benefit for this product class** and still costs credibility. When someone pitches a great joke that
has nothing to do with the bed, that is the finding to quote.

## 4. Why this is a volume problem, not a talent problem

> **[PRIMARY · Rosenbusch, Evans & Zeelenberg, "The relative importance of joke and audience
> characteristics in eliciting amusement", *Psychological Science* 33(9), 2022, pp. 1386–1394 ·
> 2026-09-07]**

| Study | Raters | Stimuli | Rater variance | **Material (the joke)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 words | 821 | 4,997 | 38.75% | **11.10%** |
| 2 jokes | 54,905 | 140 | 33.68% | **7.09%** |
| 3 cartoons | 228 | 40 | 24.31% | **8.45%** |
| **4 videos** | 118 | 120 | 23.15% | **16.55%** |

Study 5 (N=811, 105 stimuli, 85,785 ratings) adds the interaction: perceiver 20.05%, material
13.07%, **perceiver × stimulus 34.84%** — the largest single component.

**The joke explains 7–17% of amusement. The MATCH between joke and viewer explains ~35%.**

Three consequences, all of which the pipeline can act on:

1. **Never kill a comedy concept on one internal reaction.** One person not laughing is, on these
   numbers, mostly information about that person. The kill test is §1's negative-reaction question,
   which is about the ad; funniness is about the pairing.
2. **Volume and segmentation beat perfection.** This is the strongest argument in the file for the
   variance discipline already in `batch_plan.py` and `realism-qa.md` §5 — and for *persona*
   segmentation being a comedy lever, not just a targeting one.
3. **Budget for a kill ratio.** *The Onion*'s founding editor, Scott Dikkers, documents the practice:
   *"basically, 95% of everything we create is garbage"*; staff brought **20 headlines each per
   week**, and the room now reviews "several hundred headlines every week, picking only a dozen or so
   for publication" **[SECONDARY · HubSpot interview with Scott Dikkers · 2026-09-07 — doctrine, not
   evidence]**. At a 10:1 write-to-ship ratio, **shipping 30–50 comedic videos a month implies
   writing 300–500 concepts.** ⚠ That is doctrine arithmetic — one editor's account of writing
   headlines, scaled to video — and it has to keep its label all the way into a client conversation.
   It is an order of magnitude to resource against, and the reason a humorous lane
   is not a free tone toggle on a serious workflow. It converges with the variance finding above,
   which is why it is worth quoting despite being doctrine.

## 5. Construction: easy beats clever, and expected can beat surprising

**A joke that has to be worked out is a joke that dies.**

> **[PRIMARY · Gorenz & Schwarz, "Is comedy funnier when it is easier to process?", *Journal of
> Experimental Social Psychology*, 2026, art. 104970 · numbers read from a secondary summary, the
> primary is paywalled — treat the figures as SECONDARY · 2026-09-07]** Exp 1 (N=248): visually
> degraded memes rated **0.65 points less funny** on 0–6, **fully mediated** by processing ease.
> Exp 2 (N=273): the fluent (rhyming/alliterating) punchline chosen **76.45% vs 23.55%**. Exps 3–4
> (N=206, N=272): the effect is fully accounted for by fluency and coherence. **The relationship is
> linear. No inverted-U.** Difficulty subtracts.

In a feed, fluency is *already* degraded — autoplay, muted audio, a small screen, split attention.
The effect runs against you before you write a word.

**"The punchline must subvert expectation" is contradicted.**

> **[PRIMARY · Warren, Barsky & McGraw, integrative review of 20+ humour theories, *Personality and
> Social Psychology Review* 25(1), 2021 · 2026-09-07]** Surprise is **rejected as a necessary
> condition**, citing studies in which jokes with *expected* punchlines were rated funnier than
> surprising ones (Kenny 1955; Pollio & Mers 1974), and humour surviving repeated viewing.

Together these two argue for **simple, legible, product-related turns over clever ones** — which is
also what §3 requires. The three findings point the same way, which is rare enough to trust.

### The only earned seconds in this file

| Beat | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Held air BEFORE the turn** | **~1.4–1.6 s** | Professional stand-up pauses **1.37 s** before high-surprise content vs **1.01 s** before low (**+35.6%**); top-quintile performers **1.14 → 1.61 s** (**+41.2%**), bottom **0.84 → 1.07 s**. Group difference on pause behaviour **t(322) = 8.96, p < .001, d = 0.99** **[PRIMARY · Ma et al., "Timing is Everything", arXiv:2605.00143 · 828 televised stand-up performances, 86 hours, ASR transcripts · PREPRINT, not peer-reviewed, Chinese-language competition stand-up, and correlational · 2026-09-07]** |
| **Protected air AFTER the turn** | **~0.8–1.5 s** | ERP: incongruity detection N400 at **350–500 ms**, resolution P600 at **500–700 ms**, elaboration/amusement LPP at **800–1500 ms** (N=31, 90 Q/A sets) **[PRIMARY · 2026-09-07]**. Eye-tracking + pupillometry: insight ≈**800 ms** after the punchline, processing complete within ≈**1,500 ms** (N=26) **[PRIMARY · Israel, Konieczny & Ferstl, *Frontiers in Communication* 6:758173 · 2026-09-07]** |

**~2.5 s of a 35–55 s budget is spent on one joke's timing.** Cutting, pushing a caption, or starting
the next line inside the post-turn window lands on top of the resolution — the joke is *still being
processed* when you talk over it. This is the same class of error as cutting inside a settle
(`timing-and-performance.md`), and it is invisible on the page: it only exists in the edit.

⚠ **No measured maximum setup length exists for vertical video.** The experimental stimuli run
**~8–11 s** and resolve cleanly, so a setup of that length is not per se too long — but nobody has
tested it in a feed. **[UNVERIFIED]**

## 6. Serious AND funny for the same brand — the question is not the one being asked

The client's question is *"does mixing tones dilute the brand?"* **No study measures simultaneous
serious and humorous executions for one brand.** I looked; it is a genuine gap in the literature,
not a gap in the search. **[UNVERIFIED]**

The nearest measured thing reframes the question usefully:

> **[PRIMARY · Schumann, Petty & Clemons, "Predicting the effectiveness of different strategies of
> advertising variation", *Journal of Consumer Research* 17(2), 1990 · Study 1 N=294+64,
> Study 2 N=200+40 · 2026-09-07]**

| | Low involvement | **High involvement** |
|---|---|---|
| **Cosmetic** variation (same message, different look/creator/format) | **Helps** — F(1,112)=3.59, p=.06; at 4 exposures varied M=2.10 vs same M=0.93, F(1,143)=4.34, p<.04 | **Nothing.** F < 1 |
| **Substantive** variation (genuinely different arguments) | Nothing (n.s.) | **Helps** — F(1,156)=3.80, p=.05; campaign attitude F(1,76)=4.56, p<.04 |

Both benefits appeared at **moderate** repetition (4 and 3 exposures) and **disappeared at high**
repetition (8 and 5).

⛔ **A mattress is high-involvement, so cosmetic variation buys nothing.** A serious execution and a
funny execution that make **the same argument** are cosmetic variation, and are predicted to add
nothing at all. A serious execution about spinal support and a funny one about a partner stealing
the duvet are **substantive** variation, which is the supported case for exactly this involvement
level.

**So the review question for a mixed-tone batch is not "do these feel consistent?" but: *"is each of
these making a different argument, or is it the same argument wearing a different face?"*** That is
a question `batch_plan.py`'s body column already encodes — tone and body must vary *independently*,
which is precisely what the lockstep check now enforces (a batch where every funny video is body B2
is one argument told twice).

⚠ **Correction to a commonly cited source.** Schmidt & Eisend's 2015 meta-analysis on advertising
repetition (k=37, 312 effect sizes; brand attitude inverted-U peaking near **10 exposures**; recall
linear with no levelling before 8) is frequently cited for "varied executions delay wearout." **It
does not examine ad variation at all** — the authors state that content elements such as humour
"could not be examined" for want of primary data. Cite Schumann/Petty/Clemons for variation;
Schmidt & Eisend only for frequency. **[PRIMARY · 2026-09-07]**

## 7. Why AI-written comedy fails, precisely

The literature looks contradictory. It is not, and the resolution is the useful part.

**The result that says AI wins.** **[PRIMARY · Gorenz & Schwarz, "How funny is ChatGPT?", *PLOS ONE*
19(7):e0305364, 2024 · 2026-09-07]** Study 1 (producers N=105, raters N=200): ChatGPT M=2.63 vs
humans M=2.20, p<.00001; **69.5% preferred AI**; AI beat 73% / 63% / 87% of individual humans across
three tasks. Study 2 (N=217) vs published *Onion* headlines: **no significant difference** (M=2.49 vs
2.37, t(216)=1.48, p=.14).

⚠ **The design detail nearly every citation drops: ChatGPT generated 20 responses per prompt; the
humans generated 1.** That is **best-of-20 against best-of-1** — and even so it only *tied*
professional satirists. The study does not show AI jokes beat human jokes. It shows **AI volume plus
human selection** beats an unselected human attempt.

**The results that say AI fails, and name the faculty.**

> **[PRIMARY · Fettach, Bied, Toivonen & De Bie, "Cards Against LLMs", CHum 2026 · 9,612 valid
> rounds · 2026-09-07]** Models pick the funniest of ten candidates; agreement with human choice:
> Claude Opus 4.5 **17.9%**, Grok 4 17.0%, Gemini 3 Flash 16.5%, DeepSeek-V3.2 15.0%, GPT-5.2 13.4%.
> Random baseline 10%. **Popularity baseline 19.11% — every model scored below it.** Inter-model
> agreement 21.4–44.9% vs human–model 13–18%: models agree with each other far more than with people,
> converging on a *"human-misaligned notion of humor."* Content skew, models vs humans: **bodily
> 31–40% vs 21%; sexual 29–38% vs 24%; politics/society 6–8% vs 14%; identity 3–5% vs 10%.**

> **[PRIMARY · Mirowski et al., "A Robot Walks into a Bar", FAccT '24 · 20 professional comedians,
> Edinburgh Fringe 2023 · 2026-09-07]** Creativity Support Index **μ = 54.6, σ = 18.1** against a 50
> baseline. Lowest scores from stand-ups. Verbatim: *"the most bland, boring thing—I stopped reading
> it"*; *"the words seem very generic. They lack that incisiveness"*; *"AI generated material has a
> lack of agency […] lacking urgency."* Outputs skewed Western, heteronormative and male; asked for
> non-dominant voices, output was *"very artificial."*

⛔ **The failure is SELECTION, not generation.** A model cannot tell which of its own outputs is
funny to a human — measurably below a trivial popularity baseline. And left to choose, it drifts
**toward the crude and away from the observed**: for a mattress brand that predicts precisely the
wrong output — bed-and-body innuendo instead of the observational domestic material that would be
product-related (§3) and low-negative-reaction (§1).

**The pipeline the evidence supports** is the shape of the only study where AI comedy won:

> **generate at volume → a HUMAN selects → a human checks for negative reaction (§1)**

**A human being the selector is not a nice-to-have here; it is the entire finding.** The generation
step is the cheap one and can be scaled. The selection step cannot be delegated to the same model
that generated, and a second model does not fix it — models agree with *each other* two to three
times more than they agree with people.

## 8. Benign violation theory — use it to predict offence, not to be funny

The famous framework: humour arises when a circumstance is appraised as **a violation**, **benign**,
and both **simultaneously**; a violation becomes benign when another norm says it is acceptable, when
commitment to the violated norm is weak, or when the violation is **psychologically distant**
**[PRIMARY · McGraw & Warren, *Psychological Science* 21(11):1141–1149, 2010 · 2026-09-07]**.

**Read the design before leaning on it.** The five original studies use **dichotomous yes/no**
outcome measures, report **no effect sizes anywhere**, and rest on critical cells of **n ≈ 18–20**;
Study 5's manipulation is a construal-level spatial-distance prime, the genre with the worst
replication record since 2011, and no direct replication was located **[UNVERIFIED]**. Its
**necessity** claim has been falsified: **[PRIMARY · Earleywine & Mankoff, "Are benign violations
necessary for humor?", N=487 · 2026-09-07]** found three stimuli rated *funnier* than the original
scenarios while receiving **zero** "wrong" ratings (d = 0.84, 0.60, 0.58) — *"benign violations are
clearly sufficient for humor, [but] they do not seem to be a requirement."*

**What it is genuinely good for is the risk half**, and that number is the one to remember:

> **[PRIMARY · Warren & McGraw, "When does humorous marketing hurt brands?", *Journal of Marketing
> Behavior* 2:39–67, 2016 · Study 1 N=383 · 2026-09-07]** A severe violation raised humour
> **d = 0.63** — and damaged brand attitude **d = 1.36**. Study 4, 60 real print ads: violation
> severity predicting brand attitude *while controlling for humour* **b = −.23, t = −4.30, p < .001,
> partial η² = .24**. Study 2 (N=71): a violation whose target is **inclusive** improved attitudes
> (F(1,68)=5.79, p<.05, d=0.87); an **exclusive** target did not (F=0.33, n.s.).

⛔ **The brand damage of a severe violation is more than twice its humour benefit.** And the switch
between a violation that helps and one that hurts is **who the target is**: a joke the audience is
inside beats a joke the audience is outside, measurably. For a sleep brand the tempting targets —
the snoring partner, the bad sleeper, the person who cannot afford a good mattress — are all
*exclusive* targets. Aim the violation at the **situation** (the 3am ceiling, the duvet war, the
hotel pillow) rather than at a person, and the finding is on your side.

## 9. Verification — the comedy gate

Run this at script approval, not at picture lock. Ordered by what actually predicts the outcome.

> **(v2.67.0) How the negative-reaction pass is recorded.** On a job with one person deciding, ask them, with two
> fixed answers: "Nothing in it makes me cringe, or irritates, disgusts or embarrasses me" / "Something does: …".
> Name the line the cold reader found funny in the question, so a "something does" tells the writer what to cut.
> Their answer goes on the option as `comedy: asked — "<their answer>"`, and only the first answer lets the picked
> option be signed. `gate.py approve script` checks the answer against the session transcript like any approval.
> One person's answer is labelled as one person: the three people below remain the stronger test, so ask more when
> the job allows. The cold reader (`cine-cold-reader`) also says whether any line tries to be funny, so an option
> that says `comedy: none` while a stranger finds a joke is caught (`script-craft.md` §7).

- [ ] ⛔ **Negative-reaction pass** (§1): three people asked *"does any part of this produce cringe, irritation, disgust or embarrassment?"* — not *"is it funny?"*. Any yes is a rewrite, not a note
- [ ] ⛔ **Relatedness test** (§3): the joke is about the product's world (sleep, bed, morning, back, partner). If the joke would work for a different product, it is unrelated humour on a white good and buys nothing
- [ ] ⛔ **Target test** (§8): the violation is aimed at the **situation**, not at a person the audience could be
- [ ] **Fluency test** (§5): a viewer with the sound off and half their attention gets it on the first pass. Nothing has to be worked out (on the cut, this is T1 and T2 of `sound-off-story.md`)
- [ ] **Timing marked in the script** (§5): ~1.5 s of held air before the turn; ~1 s protected after it, with no cut, caption push or next line inside that window
- [ ] **Substantive, not cosmetic** (§6): this execution makes a *different argument* from the serious one it sits beside — not the same argument with a joke on top
- [ ] **A human selected it** from a larger set (§7). If the shortlist was model-picked, the finding says the selection is below a popularity baseline — re-pick
- [ ] Written-to-shipped ratio recorded for the batch (§4). Without it, "we need more concepts" stays an opinion
- [ ] Claims read again *after* the comedy pass — a joke that lands on a benefit has quietly made a claim (`compliance.md`)

## 10. Blacklist

1. **"90% of people are more likely to remember a funny ad" / "91% prefer brands to be funny" /
   "72% would choose a brand that uses humour."** All from the Oracle *Happiness Report* 2022 — a
   paid opt-in Savanta panel, n=12,183, stated preference, not behaviour. The memory claim is
   **directly contradicted by measurement**: Eisend's brand recall r = .071, CI [−.050, .190], n.s.
   **[PRIMARY · Oracle/PR Newswire release with methodology · 2026-09-07]**
2. **"Funny ads are more memorable"** — any phrasing, any percentage. All four of Eisend's
   memory rows are non-significant. Humour buys attention and liking, not memory (§2).
3. **The "vampire effect" applied to humour.** Category error. The measured vampire effect is about
   **celebrity endorsers** — **φ = .05–.09**, roughly a 5-point recall penalty, across N=4,970
   **[PRIMARY · Erfgen, Zenker & Sattler, *IJRM* 32(2), 2015 · 2026-09-07]**, mitigated by
   celebrity–brand congruence and **not** by brand familiarity. There is no equivalent measurement
   for humour; Eisend 2011's version is a modelling interpretation whose coefficients are paywalled
   and **[UNVERIFIED]**.
4. **Schmidt & Eisend 2015 cited for "variety delays wearout."** The paper does not examine
   variation (§6). Use Schumann/Petty/Clemons 1990, and carry its involvement interaction with it.
5. **Berger & Milkman 2012 cited for "funny content goes viral."** Two separate objections, and
   `hook-craft.md` §7 raises only the first: (a) the outcome measure is *being emailed a New York
   Times article*, which is not being watched; (b) amusement is not in the field coefficient table — awe 0.34, anger 0.38, anxiety 0.24, sadness −0.17, positivity 0.16. Amusement
   appears only in a lab study, N=49, as an arousal manipulation, with *emailing a New York Times
   article* as the outcome. The two files quote different rows of the same table because they are
   objecting to different things — not a disagreement.
6. **"The rule of three."** **No empirical test located.** The only academic item is a *conceptual
   framework* paper. Doctrine — label it as doctrine, and note that no measured work exists on
   setup:punchline ratio, punchline position within a short video, or tag/topper structure either
   **[UNVERIFIED]**.
7. **"The punchline must surprise."** Contradicted (§5).
8. **"Clever is funnier."** Contradicted — fluency is linear, with no inverted-U (§5).
9. **Kantar's "53% of ads used humour in 2000 → 34% in 2024"** and its "impact multiplier."
   Directionally interesting, **method undisclosed**: no database size, no sampling, no humour-coding
   scheme, no number behind the multiplier. Market context, never evidence. **[TERTIARY · 2026-09-07]**
10. **Any fixed-seconds comedy prescription** beyond the two in §5. Everything else circulating is
    invented — see `hook-craft.md` §7 on the "3-second rule" for the same pattern.

## 11. What this file cannot tell you

- **How to be funny.** Nothing here generates a joke. §1, §3 and §8 tell you which jokes to *kill*,
  which is a different and more tractable service — and the one the evidence actually supports.
- **Whether a specific joke will land.** The joke is 7–17% of the variance (§4). No pre-test at this
  scale distinguishes a good joke from a bad one reliably, and `performance-metrics.md` §0's
  prediction ceiling applies here in full.
- **Anything about comedy delivered by a SYNTHETIC performer.** Not measured anywhere. Given §7's
  homogenisation finding and the timing dependencies in §5 — which a generated clip controls badly
  (`timing-and-performance.md`) — this is the highest-value open question for this pipeline and
  should be treated as an experiment, not a capability. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- **Non-US audiences.** 41 of Eisend's 43 studies are US-based, and humour is the most
  culture-bound thing in this skill.
