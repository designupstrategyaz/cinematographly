# The move catalogue — specific things that were shot, kept, and can be asked for by name

⚠️ **Read this catalogue with `specifics-catalogue.md` §10.3, and read the number the right way round.** Across five measured festival AI shorts, **42% of shots are locked off and six in ten carry no deliberate camera move** — so about **four in ten do move**, and pans and tilts outnumber pushes three to one. That is a mix, not a default. A catalogue of 62 usable named moves (§34, §35, §39 and §40 are reserved for house decisions) is a menu, not a quota: it guards against the slow push-in on everything, and the opposite failure — a locked frame on everything — is just as dead. Every move here earns its place by a job (`cinematography-craft.md` §3.8, lever 5) and by where it falls in the sequence's camera plan (`sequence-grammar.md` §2b).

⚠️ **SUPERSEDES IN PLACE** (2026-10-04) — previously: "A catalogue of 33 named moves is a menu, not a quota: the commonest choice in films that work is to hold the frame." Read as an instruction, that line helped produce 97 % locked-off shots across 20 ads written with the skill (`cinematography-craft.md` §3.8 has the lab-00005 figures).

⚡ **This file holds CAMERA MOVES. Its sibling `specifics-catalogue.md` holds the other five
categories** — dramaturgy, sequences, prop design, scripting and transitions — as fragments
fragments. If what you are looking for is a beat structure, a shot chain, a prop as it was written,
a line, or how one shot handed to the next, it is there, not here.

⛔ **What this file is, and what the other files are.** `cinematography-craft.md` §3.9 indexes
*signal → move class* ("significance approaching → push in"). That is one abstraction too high to
execute. **This file holds the actual moves**: named, with the numbers, with what they do and what
breaks them. A director asks for "the centred pivot whip", not for "a pan".

⚠️ **The evidence bar here is lower than elsewhere in this skill, and it is stated rather than
hidden.** These come from practitioner corpora — a set of production prompts with their revision
history, and a paid course's full build of one short film with its rejected takes. **"Worked" means
the author shot it, looked at it and kept it**, not that anything was measured.
`[SECONDARY · practitioner corpus]` on every entry unless marked otherwise.
Treat them as a repertoire to choose from, not as claims about what performs. When one of these is
used and the client's numbers come back, that result belongs in `memory/episodes/`, and *then* the
entry gets a measurement.

**How to use it.** Find the dramatic job in the left column of the index, read the entry, paste the
spec into the prompt, and keep the name in the shotlist so the next person knows what was intended.

⛔ **Every entry names the kind of source it came from.** The first pass of this file drew **10 of
13 moves from one film** — a single drama — and nothing at all from the dialogue comedy in the same
corpus. A catalogue built from one genre teaches one genre. The provenance line is there so
the next person can see the imbalance instead of inheriting it.

## 1. The centred pivot whip

**Spec.** Plant the camera *between* the two people, midway, pivoting on the spot — not at either
shoulder. Settle on A in CU. Hold. **Settled until ~0.3 s before the move · a ~0.5 s motion-blur
swing · B settled by ~1.4 s.** Same shot size both ends.

**What it does.** A cut lets the viewer off; a whip does not. Because the move is continuous, the
reaction is *unavoidable* — you are turned toward it rather than shown it. It is the right move for
a question whose answer is a face.

**Breaks when.** The frame size changes mid-swing — medium in, close-up out. That is a disguised cut
and reads as a glitch (`prompt_lint.py` blocks it). Also when the timings are left out: without the
numbers the pacing is guessed and the swing either lags or lands before the face has settled.

**Amendment (2026-10-29): two measured whip profiles.** Beside "a ~0.5 s motion-blur swing": the
kinetic engine's join — out in 0.12–0.13 s on an accelerating curve and in over the same time on a
decelerating one, the cut in the middle, a ±2° roll, the shutter stretched to about 600°, the whoosh
0.12 s before the cut, horizontal and vertical whips alternating `[A·s · SC 2.9]`; and the course's
operator whip — onset 16 → 113 %/s in 0.13 s, 0.71 s of blur with the join inside it, settled 0.1 s later,
the subject the same size on both sides (0.78 against 0.79–0.83 of the height) `[C·m · course notes]`. As a
join, check: a ramp up of 0.10–0.15 s; 0.5–0.8 s of blur across the cut; the same direction both sides;
settled within 0.15 s; subject size matched within ±10 %. A hyperlapse spin whip ran about 440 %/s for
1 s and dimmed to near black before its cut `[C·m · course notes]`. The cap of two whips a film stands (C5).
Tags as in the legend before §34.

## 2. The worm's-eye rise

**Spec.** Open at ground level, lens almost on the asphalt, tight on an object and the feet moving
toward it. As the subject arrives, **crane up in one unbroken restless move** to a low-then-eye-level
framing on the face, drifting closer as they speak. Dutch tilt on the rise. Subject held hard
off-centre throughout.

**What it does.** The camera performs the thing the character has not said. Starting at defeat-level
and ending on a lit face *is* the recovery — which is why it works as a closing shot and why the
dialogue over it can stay ordinary.

**Breaks when.** It is centred. The whole move reads as a reveal, and a centred reveal reads as an
advert.

**Amendment (2026-10-29): two measured relatives.** The course's intro tilt on a monopod opens at
headlight height on the legs, tilts up (with a small rise) 35 % of the height in 4.0 s at about 9 %/s
(6 → 11 → 7), float 2.2, the face resolving about 2.9 s in, then pans 28 % in 2.5 s and holds 0.9 s
locked — and he arrives centre-right, which agrees with the rule above `[C·m · course notes]`. The motorised
dolly tilt runs low over the ground, then snaps up 32–71 % of the height at 74–96 %/s for about 0.5 s,
settled within 0.75 s, then holds 1.5–2 s; it is obtained as two clips joined mid-move, or as a first and
last frame from ground level to the low angle `[C·m · course notes]`. That one ends centred (x 0.45–0.57),
the course's brand-intro form: recorded here, not adopted — off-centre stands for drama.

## 3. The blind open

**Spec.** **First 2.5 s: 100% opaque** — one colour of dust or fog, edge to edge, zero readable
shapes, only churning texture. **2.5 → 4.5 s: thin to 30%** in stages — a light gradient first, then
a soft edge, then a crisp line. **The subject enters only after the location is readable.** Dissolve
by dilution (thinning evenly everywhere), never by parting like a curtain.

**What it does.** Buys a reveal you have not paid for anywhere else, and gates it on *state* rather
than on the clock — the place resolves, then the person arrives, so the viewer has somewhere to put
them.

**Breaks when.** The subject is in frame one. Then it is a foggy shot, not an opening.

## 4. The split OTS pair

**Spec.** Two reverse over-the-shoulders, **one line each**, with the pause living *between the
shots* rather than inside one. From the course: one short line of admission in the first single —
cut — a two-part line in the reverse, with the held pause between its halves. Level, un-canted,
locked off, 50mm, the 180° line held.

**What it does.** A single take carrying both lines plays as if in one breath. Splitting gives the
audience the processing beat, and it gives you two takes to pick from per line.

**Breaks when.** You try to buy the pause with a longer clip instead of another shot. The model
renders only what is written, so unwritten air is simply absent.

## 5. The lip-ECU rise

**Spec.** Open in ECU **on the mouth** for a single spoken word, then **rise slowly to the
eyes** in one continuous move on the operator axis and hold. 3/4 from the shadow side: near cheek
in shadow, window light rimming the far edge, wet catchlights carrying the eyes.

**What it does.** Separates the word from the feeling and orders them. The word lands with no face
attached to it; the rise is what delivers the face, so the emotion arrives as a consequence of the
word rather than alongside it.

**Breaks when.** It starts wide. Then the rise has nowhere to go.

## 6. The doorway silhouette

**Spec.** A door opens into a dark interior; **hard daylight ~3 stops over the interior exposure**
blasts through. The newcomer is a **pure black silhouette**, face unreadable, edges rimmed.
Volumetric shafts fan past them through the dust, striping the floor.

**What it does.** Interrupts a scene without spending a face on it. Identity is withheld and the
interruption still lands, which is exactly what you want when the newcomer is not the point.

**Breaks when.** The exposure is balanced. A visible face turns the interruption into a character
introduction, and you now owe that character something.

## 7. The deep-to-near rack

**Spec.** Camera almost on the ground. Focus sits on the **far** element while it does something
(a distant figure moving, getting ready), then **racks forward** to a foreground
detail ~20 cm from the lens as a hand sets it down.

**What it does.** Ties the two ends of a geography together in one uncut shot. The viewer learns the
distance *and* the stake without an establishing wide, which is why it is worth more than the wide
it replaces.

**Breaks when.** Both planes are sharp. Deep focus makes it a static two-plane frame and the rack
was the whole idea.

**Amendment (2026-10-29): the rack's timing.** The course's racks complete in about 1.3 s, soft to
sharp, with the face size flat (±3 %): a band of 0.8–1.3 s, a subject size change ≤ 5 %, translation
≤ 4 % `[C·m · course notes]`. Blocking's default `rack` of 0.8 s fits. A rack is F as text
(`cinematography-craft.md` §3.8): test it in a lab before relying on it; the fallback is two frames or a
post blur pass over 0.8–1.3 s. ⚠️ Focus breathing reads to a tracker as a 4–18 % zoom: gate a rack's QA
on the subject's size, never on the tracker's scale.

## 8. The anti-lookroom pin

**Spec.** Subject **hard against the frame edge**, face almost touching it, **looking off that edge**
— no space in the direction of the gaze. All open space behind them. ⚠️ **State it more than once**:
models regress to the convention and will re-centre the subject or flip the space.

**What it does.** Conventional lookroom says a person has somewhere to go. Removing it says they do
not. It is unease built out of composition rather than performance, so the actor can stay still.

**Breaks when.** It is used more than once in a film. It is a strong effect with no second gear.

## 9. The strike ramp

**Spec.** On an already-locked continuous take, add one instruction: **dip to slow motion as the
object travels, snap back to full speed on the consequence.** Not a whole-shot slow-mo — the ramp is
the point.

**What it does.** Slows the exact frames the character will replay for the rest of their life, and
returns to real speed for the part the world experienced. The subjective and objective versions of
one second, inside one shot.

**Breaks when.** There is no locked take under it. ⛔ A speed ramp asked for cold, with no
established shot under it, has no beat to ramp around. The terse revision
works **because** the base shot already exists.

## 10. The broadcast interruption

**Spec.** In a sequence of handheld documentary singles, make **one** shot an **8° super-tele
(~300–400 mm)** anchored far away at the edge of the action, with the same 1–2 cm tremor. It **does not
approach and does not cross** — it only reframes within its own vantage. Heavy compression, the air
column flattened, only the action sharp.

**What it does.** Switches the viewer from participant to spectator for one shot, which is what makes
a private moment read as a public event. The handheld shots around it are what give it meaning.

**Breaks when.** It is used more than once, or the camera moves position. Then it is just a long lens.

## 11. The reused frame

**Spec.** Take a frame from a generation you already paid for and use it as a held shot — most often
as the **1–2 s processing beat** after a flashback, before the next line.

**What it does.** Solves a pacing problem for **zero credits**. It is the cheapest move in this file
and the one most often forgotten, because the instinct after a note is to generate something.

**Breaks when.** The frame carries motion. A held frame from a moving shot reads as a freeze, which
is a different and louder device.

## 12. The isolated read

**Spec.** When a locked shot has the right picture and the wrong delivery, **do not re-roll it.**
Generate a **face-only clip in a quiet setup**, direct the delivery, and marry the audio in the edit.
Direct it as a read, not as a scene — small pauses, a trace of self-reproach, an unsteady voice —
and **name the word the voice breaks on**.

**What it does.** Separates a performance defect from a picture that is already right. On a 20-second
action take this is the difference between one cheap generation and re-rolling everything.

**Breaks when.** The problem is actually the line. A read cannot rescue writing.

## 13. The frozen-frame handoff

**Spec.** Attach the **last frame of the previous scene** as the opening reference for the next, so
the new setup starts from that exact frozen position — same pose, same light, same instant, new
camera. Generate at the higher tier: atmospherics (flares, fog) are smoothed away at lower
resolutions before they reach the master. `[UNVERIFIED — the resolution claim is asserted by the
source and not measured; it is on the open-test list in prompting-craft.md §11e]`

**What it does.** Makes a cut between two separately generated scenes invisible, which is the join
this pipeline breaks on most often.

**Breaks when.** The two scenes disagree about light. The handoff frame carries the old scene's key,
so a lighting change has to happen somewhere else.

## 14. The ceiling drop

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** Camera **locked off below the top line of the
surface the subject lands on**, 1 m out, looking slightly up, hard **15° Dutch**. The subject falls
into the static frame from above the top edge — **frame one already has the body breaking that
edge**. The landing surface compresses deep, something loose bounces off, dust bursts into the
light.

**What it does.** A locked camera makes an arrival violent. If the camera moves to meet him the
energy is shared between them; if it refuses to move, all of it lands on the body. It also puts the
inciting incident in frame one, which is the whole hook problem solved by blocking.

**Breaks when.** There is an establishing frame before the drop. The empty room tells the audience to
settle, and then the joke has to wake them up again.

## 15. The chair-back reveal

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** Open the segment on the **backrest** of a swivel
chair facing camera, occupant hidden behind it. The chair then **rotates 180°** with believable
inertia and **a small overshoot** as it stops, revealing the occupant already looking past the lens
at the other character.

**What it does.** Withholds a character for a beat *inside one continuous shot* — no cut, so no
escape. The overshoot is what makes it read as furniture rather than as a camera trick, and the fact
that her eyeline is already locked when she arrives says she was waiting.

**Breaks when.** The chair rotates at constant speed and stops dead. Then it is a turntable.

## 16. The mirrored Dutch

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** Hard **15° Dutch left** on one character's
coverage, hard **15° Dutch right — mirrored** — on the other's. Level horizon returns for the third
shot, where they share the frame.

**What it does.** Two people in one destabilised world, and the mirroring makes them read as
*opposed* rather than merely tilted. Returning to level when they finally share a frame is the
resolution, done with the horizon instead of with dialogue.

**Breaks when.** Both singles cant the same way. Then it is a house style, not a relationship.

## 17. The 5cm threat

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** A finger arched **5 cm above a button**, held
**for the entire scene**, the gap stated as nothing but air, with exactly **one tiny waggle** as its
only movement, and the press withheld until the last beat. The threatening hand stays **close to the
chest, elbow bent, never extended** toward the other character.

**What it does.** The stakes of the whole scene live in a five-centimetre gap. Tension from an action
*not* taken costs nothing to generate and cannot drift, because nothing is happening — the model has
one static relationship to hold rather than a motion to invent. ⚡ The un-extended arm is the other
half: extending the threat would make her plead; keeping it close makes her certain.

**Breaks when.** The finger drifts or hovers *around*. State the gap as a measurement and restate it
in the locks, or the hand lands on the button early.

## 18. The repeated identical single

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** Each character's lines are shot from **one
framing, reused exactly** — her singles always identical, his always the same over-shoulder. Shot 3
repeats shot 1's framing; shot 4 repeats shot 2's. Locked off, no reframing inside any of them.

**What it does.** When the camera stops offering anything new, the audience reads faces. It is the
correct default for a two-person scene where the comedy is in the performances, and it makes the one move
you *do* spend — a whip, a push — land far harder than it would in a restless cut.

**Breaks when.** You "improve" one of the repeats. A repeat that is nearly the same reads as an
error; identical reads as a choice.

## 19. The persistent live element

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** One live thing in the background
(an animal, say), **present in every shot where its position is visible**, doing small idle
business — a stir, a head turn, a slow blink, a shift of weight — and reacting once at the big beat.
Never leaves, never absent.

**What it does.** Two jobs at once. The room stays alive without spending a cut, and it becomes a
**continuity anchor**: an element that must be present and identical in every shot is a thing that
fails loudly when the model re-improvises the room.

**Breaks when.** It performs. The moment the background element gets a joke of its own it stops being
background and starts competing.

## 20. The uniform dissolve

**From:** a dialogue comedy in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** Opacity drops **evenly head-to-toe, all at
once** — not bottom-up, not a wipe. Particles lift off the silhouette and drift up. **The
character's sound decays in exact sync with the body's fade.** The contact shadow fades with the
body; whatever was carrying its weight slowly recovers as the weight leaves it. The face is the last
thing to go.

**What it does.** Makes an impossible event obey physics, which is the only reason it reads instead
of looking like a filter. ⚡ **The audio sync and the recovering surface are what sell it** — the
world reacts to the absence, so the absence is real.

**Breaks when.** The fade is bottom-up or the sound holds at full volume. Either one turns it into a
transition effect.

## 21. The split reaction

**From:** an action sequence in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** Three characters' heads **snap
toward a distant sound in one crisp synchronised whip**, bodies otherwise frozen — while one other
element in frame (an animal, say) **carries on with its idle business, oblivious**. Locked off,
deadpan, no camera move.

**What it does.** Comedy from one element in frame refusing to acknowledge the event everyone else
reacted to. The synchronisation of the people is what makes the other element's indifference funny;
if they reacted raggedly there would be no unison to break.

**Breaks when.** Everything reacts. Or the camera moves — a move tells the audience what to feel, and
deadpan requires them to decide.

## 22. The single column

**From:** a large-scale action sequence in the practitioner corpus. **Spec.** A huge attacker
fires, and the payoff is **ONE** tall column of spray erupting 30 m short of the target. It hangs,
collapses, drifts away. The attacker carries on — **no hit, no damage, no fire.** Hold the wide long,
longer than the shots that preceded it.

**What it does.** Scale through restraint. One column in a held wide reads bigger than a barrage,
because the audience measures the miss against the size of the thing that missed. The long hold is
half the move: the shot has to outlast the event.

**Breaks when.** There are two columns, or the shot cuts on the splash. Both read as coverage of an
action scene rather than as a beat.

## 23. The seeded transformation

**From:** the Seedance 4K practitioner corpus, its v2v set — the same scaffold ran three times over
one plate (cybernetic, molten, invisible), which is what makes it a template rather than a shot.

**Spec.** Four parts, in this order:
1. **Seed it on something already in the plate.** All three versions start at **the subject's
   existing tattoo**, whose lines begin to glow. The effect begins at a real feature of the footage,
   so its first frame is already grounded.
2. **Write an ORDER, not a state.** Seams open **section by section**; parts seat **one at a time**,
   each named (a servo, a cable, a lit strip); plates unfold and lock **plate by plate**; then each
   finger is covered **knuckle by knuckle**.
3. **Name a direction of travel.** Wrist → forearm → back of the hand → fingers. The transformation
   has a front that moves across the limb.
4. **Name the terminal state.** By the end the hand and forearm are fully transformed, and the rest
   of the body and the room are unchanged.

**What it does.** A transformation written as a state ("his arm becomes metal") gives the model one
instruction and 240 frames to fill however it likes — which is where the morphing happens. Written as
an ordered sequence with a seed and a terminus, every frame has a defined position in the progression.
⚡ **And the scaffold is material-agnostic**: the molten version swaps *seams and servos* for *cracks
and magma*, the invisible version for *glassy refraction*, and the order, seed and terminus stay
identical. Write it once, reuse it.

**Breaks when.** The seed is invented rather than found in the plate. An effect that starts from
nothing has no anchor and the first frame is already a comp.

## 24. The named ramp

**From:** five spectacle prompts in the Seedance 4K practitioner corpus (creature, POV and
game-cinematic) — all five.

**Spec.** Three parts, and the corpus never omits one:
1. **Declare the default.** No slow motion unless stated; the one named pass is the only speed
   shift; slow motion on one named action only. Without this the model ramps everywhere and the
   whole shot turns to syrup.
2. **Name the trigger frame**, not a time range — a near-collision, an impact, a single blow.
3. ⚡ **Write what the ramp REVEALS.** This is the part that is actually the move: debris or
   particles hanging frozen between two subjects; sparks suspended in mid-air with every speck sharp;
   a huge eye with a tiny figure reflected in it.

**What it does.** A ramp is not a speed instruction. **It is a shot that only exists at that speed** —
suspended debris, a reflection you could not otherwise hold on, a pupil. If you cannot name what the
audience gets to look at during the ramp, you do not need the ramp.

**Breaks when.** The default is unstated, or the ramp has no content. A slow-motion of nothing is the
most common way a spectacle shot reads as a demo.

**Amendment (2026-10-29): two more corpora declare the default, and where the slow motion comes
from.** The game trailers keep real-time speed and never slow comedy down; the same-moves base takes ask
for real-speed motion, never slow motion and never stop-motion (SC 4.15, 5.15). On a generated 24 fps
clip a 40 % ramp shows repeated frames — it invents 1.5 of every 2.5 — so ramp only a 60 fps source (on
Higgsfield the upscaler is the only place to set fps) or retime with optical flow and strip-check it; the
course's result reveals ramp to 40 % over about 1.5–2 s `[C·m · course notes]`. Whether a
whole-clip HFR conform of real footage is exempt from the ramp cap is an open owner question
(Appendix P, P15).

## 25. The pinned screen-space layer

**From:** the two game-cinematic prompts in the practitioner corpus.

**Spec.** Describe the UI **element by element with its screen position and a hex colour** —
top-centre compass, top-right minimap, right-edge quest log, bottom-left level badge and bars,
bottom-centre hotbar, bottom-right ammo. Then the two locks: **flat 2D in screen space, never placed
in the 3D world**, and **pinned in every shot — the numbers may change, the layout may not**.

**What it does.** A model handed "a game HUD" will draw UI *into* the scene — on walls, floating in
depth, re-arranged per cut. Naming screen-space explicitly, plus a per-element layout, is what keeps
it a layer. ⚡ **The same two locks apply to any overlay** — captions, lower thirds, a price card:
`typography-and-captions.md` owns the design, this owns the instruction that stops it drifting into
the world.

**Breaks when.** The layout is described as a vibe. "A sci-fi HUD" produces a different HUD per cut,
which is the most visible continuity failure available, because the audience reads UI as text.

## 26. The bridge shot (the teleport fix)

*Provenance: course notes.*

**Spec.** A character or vehicle ends shot A at one location and must begin shot C at another. Do
not cut. Insert **shot B: a short passage in NEITHER place** — the road between, a corridor, a
stairwell, the inside of the car with only sky outside. Shot B's plate is deliberately
*uninformative*: it must not contain the destination.

**What it does.** A hard cut between two locations reads as teleportation, because a generated
sequence gives the viewer none of the continuity cues (matching light, consistent props, a
recognisable geography) that let a live-action cut carry a jump. One shot of transit converts the
jump into travel. It is cheap — the bridge frame is usually the easiest shot in the sequence.

**Breaks when.** ⚡ **The bridge uses the destination's plate** — then it does not bridge, it
*spoils*, and the arrival lands as a repeat rather than a reveal. The whole value of the shot is
that it shows motion without showing where the motion ends. Also breaks when the bridge is replaced
by a close-up: a CU of a face hides the geography but does not advance it, so the jump is still
there, just less visible.

## 27. The statics pull — matching stills derived from one motion source

*Provenance: course notes.*

**Spec.** You need several stills of one subject that must agree with each other — a product from
four angles, a cabin from three positions, a character's coverage. ⛔ Do **not** generate them
separately. Generate **one continuous "statics" clip** that moves around the subject, then **pause
on the useful frames and save each as a named element**.

**What it does.** Frames from one continuous generation are physically obliged to agree: same
object, same light, same wear, same reflections, same time of day. Independently generated stills
are four separate negotiations about the same subject and they will disagree in exactly the places
nobody checks — a trim line, a badge position, a shade of grey. This also produces sub-references
cheaply (`character-and-props.md` §8.3b): the course's interior-detail sub-reference came from
pausing such a clip.

**Breaks when.** The motion clip itself drifts — a slow orbit that morphs the subject halfway
through yields frames that disagree *and* a false sense that they cannot. Screen the clip for drift
before pulling frames from it. Also breaks when the move is too fast: motion blur costs you the
detail you were pulling the frame for.

## 28. The unambiguous first setup

*Provenance: course notes.*

**Spec.** When a scene has several people or objects whose spatial relationship matters, shoot the
**least ambiguous angle first** — the flat, boring, everything-visible one — **even though it is not
the most dramatic**. Approve it. *Then* generate the coverage, binding that frame.

**What it does.** Positions get locked by the first approved frame whether you meant them to or not;
everything after inherits them. Starting with the dramatic angle locks a geography you cannot read,
so the coverage inherits an ambiguity and every later shot argues with a different reading of it.
The dull frame is a **cheap continuity instrument**, not a shot you have to use in the cut.

**Breaks when.** The unambiguous setup is treated as the opening shot of the sequence. It is
scaffolding — it may well not appear in the edit at all. Also breaks when it is skipped because "the
positions are obvious": they are obvious to you, who has the script, and invisible to the model.

**Amendment (2026-10-29): the same scaffolding in two more corpora.** The dialogue skill makes one
two-shot of the tallest and the smallest character together only to lock their relative scale, and the
blockout skill stores its coverage cameras as specs relative to the subject, so every angle of a beat
shares the same frames (SC 1.30, 6.20) — scaffolding, not a shot.

## 29. The survivor anchor

*Provenance: course notes.*

**Spec.** When a sequence keeps failing, find the shot that **survived every take** — the one that
came back usable on attempt after attempt — and rebuild the sequence *around* it rather than
continuing to fight the shots that keep failing. In the course build this was one effects shot; it
became the anchor and the neighbouring shots were re-designed to hand off to it.

**What it does.** A shot that reliably generates is telling you something: its dependencies are few
and its action is legible to the model. That is information about what this scene *can* be, and it
is more reliable than your storyboard, which was written without it. Rebuilding around the survivor
spends the sequence's complexity budget where it actually works.

**Breaks when.** The survivor is anchored for *reliability* rather than for what it *means*. A shot
that generates beautifully and says nothing will happily become the centre of a sequence about
nothing. The survivor earns the anchor slot only if it carries a beat the story needs.

## 30. The asphalt-skim head-on near-miss

*Provenance: course notes, a high-speed vehicle sequence, cut 1 — a 3-second beat.*

**Spec.** Open **inches above the asphalt**, camera hurtling forward, lane markings whipping beneath
it as white streaks. **Climb along the direction of flight** so the horizon snaps up and reveals the
hero car already close, coming **straight at the camera in the camera's own lane**. The nose fills
frame within a breath; **at the last moment the car snaps one sharp lane change** into the
neighbouring lane of its own side and sweeps past. ⛔ **The camera holds its glide throughout.**

**What it does.** Buys a genuine flinch in three seconds, and the flinch is **free of cutting** — the
collision is avoided by the *subject* moving, not by the camera dodging or by an edit. A camera that
holds its line while the world swerves reads as fearless; a camera that swerves reads as an accident.

**Breaks when.** The camera dodges. Also when the avoidance is a vague swerve rather than **one lane
change into a named lane** — an unspecified dodge puts the car into oncoming traffic and breaks the
scene's lane law (`specifics-catalogue.md` §2.9). And when the climb is described as a tilt: the
camera must **rise along its flight path**, so the reveal comes from altitude, not from pointing up.

## 31. The reflection tag-chase

*Provenance: same sequence, cut 2.*

**Spec.** The **entire frame lives inside a mirrored building facade** — nothing is shot directly.
Camera slides fast along the glass; the reflected car enters from behind, **chases, gains, draws
level** until it fills most of frame, then **out-accelerates the camera and slides out of the
reflection**. Other traffic glides through the mirror behind it in its own lanes. A **dense
out-of-focus foreground** — passers-by, a cyclist, poles, planters, café furniture — whips past as
motion-blur streaks. Handheld, with a hard, jittery, near-documentary shake.

**What it does.** Three jobs at once. It is a speed shot (the car beats the camera). It is a location
shot (the city is in the glass). And ⚡ **it makes the cabin unviewable by construction** — a
reflection has no interior, so identity cannot drift where there is no face (`specifics-catalogue.md`
§1.9). The mullions of the facade slice the car into vertical bands, which reads as graphic design
rather than as artefact.

**Breaks when.** The reflection is treated as a look rather than as the frame. Asking for a car
reflected in a glass building gives you a car with a building behind it; the instruction that works
is that **the image IS the reflection** — the whole frame lives inside the mirrored facade. Also breaks
without the foreground layer: a clean reflection reads as a flat composite, and the whipping
out-of-focus junk is what supplies parallax.

## 32. The pinned-detail orbital

*Provenance: same sequence, cut 3.*

**Spec.** Match the subject's speed alongside it, then **arc in one smooth orbital move around a
single named detail** — here a headlight — with **the detail staying pinned in frame while the
perspective wraps around it**. Neighbouring features rotate through the composition; the ground
below smears to pure motion blur; traffic streams past as soft blurs.

**What it does.** A beauty shot that is also a motion shot. Pinning one small feature gives the
orbit a centre the viewer can hold onto, so the move reads as deliberate rather than as drift — and
it puts the product detail (the thing the sheet was written for) at the exact centre of the frame for
the length of the shot.

**Breaks when.** The pinned detail is not in the reference sheet. This move resolves one feature for
a full beat, so anything vague there mutates in the open — which is the argument in
`specifics-catalogue.md` §3.2 for describing the attributes a lens will actually reach. Also breaks
if two details are named: the orbit needs exactly one centre.

**Amendment (2026-10-29): an ending, and measured relatives.** Scenario's product orbit ends by pushing
into a macro of one detail — about 180° of orbit with a push over 0–5 s, then into a single drop on the
label over 5–8 s — and closes with an **ending-stability block**: the letters, the drop and the droplets
fixed, nothing morphing, the last second a calm settle. It was used from 0.5 s (173 frames), and five
later edits kept its move frame for frame `[A·m · SC 1.20 · minimax_h3 at 2K]`. The block is a prompt
answer to the settle the plugin measured as missing (`timing-and-performance.md` §5, §7) — worth an arm
in the next test. The course confirms the mechanism on people: a standing subject pinned within ±0.03 of
the width and ±6 % in size while the background runs 7–10 %/s `[C·m · course notes]`; the slider's short
form is §61. ⛔ The ads-lane caution on slow orbits (`motion-craft.md` §7b) stands, and the orbit forms
wait in Appendix P (P10).

## 33. The camera as a second participant

*Provenance: practitioner corpus (Seedance 2.0) — two creature oners, one of them FPV.*

**Spec.** In an unbroken take, give the camera a **role in the scene rather than a position**: it is
another flyer, another rider, another diver. It obeys the same physics as the subject, has its own
reactions, and never assumes an impossible vantage.

In the corpus: the camera orbits and chases **as a second flyer**; in the other oner it is an FPV
presence **flying with the creature**; and on a POV shot it flinches with the character — **a small
jerk back and to the right**.

**What it does.** Solves the hardest problem in a long generated take — what the camera does next.
A camera with a *role* has an answer at every moment; a camera with a *list of moves* runs out and
starts wandering. It also produces the reactions for free: a second flyer banks when the air
changes, flinches at a near-miss, and falls behind when the subject accelerates.

**Breaks when.** The role is physically impossible for the shot. A "second flyer" cannot be inside
the cabin. Also breaks when the role is stated and then contradicted by a move only a crane could
make — the corpus keeps them consistent, naming the two rigs whose motion it wants (an FPV
quadcopter and a robotic arm).

**Amendment (2026-10-29): the action variants, measured.** The struck operator (§51) and the
ride-along roll (§52) are this entry's fight versions, from the course's measured fight (course notes).
Drama and action only.

breathing as scale, so push and zoom amounts below come from the subject's size where the course says so.

**A music-video family, beside them** `[PRACTITIONER · course notes · measured from a 360p decode]`, from the same

## 36. The counter dive

*Provenance: Scenario's kinetic-film engine (engine/blocks.js: the `zoom` exit of `Words`, the
`Graph` portal, the `Prompt` push into Enter), SC 2.5, 1.23, 3.12 `[A·s]`; the live-action form is the
course's credited hyperlapse transition, course notes `[C·m]`; the hidden cut "match on black" is
`editing-and-audio.md`.*

**For.** Carry the eye from a word, a doorway or a button straight into the next shot.

**Spec.** End a shot by flying into a dark or solid opening that belongs to the subject, and start the
next shot inside it. Three forms:
1. **A letter's counter** — the hole of an O, D, 0, A, B, P or R in the last word: scale ×70 on an
   accelerating curve (ease-in exponential) over about 0.3 s (0.9 beat at 172 BPM), re-centring on the
   counter as it grows, with a long-shutter smear (×2.2) and a whoosh at its start `[A·s]`. The number
   form: the figure lands ×2.8 → 1 in 0.22 s (ease-out, no overshoot) with a dark disc in its counter,
   and the dive goes through the disc `[A·s]`.
2. **A dark doorway or gate**, real or generated: mean luma falls 76 → 14 (of 255) in 0.3 s at the
   opening, and the cut sits inside the dark `[C·m · course notes]`; the push into it ran ×2.1 in 1.54 s
   (63 %/s) `[C·m]`.
3. **A solid colour:** push into a key, a button or a swatch until its colour fills the frame on the
   last beat — ×2.6 (ease in-out), then ×16 (ease-in exponential) — and open the next shot on the same
   colour `[A·s]`.

Accelerate into the opening; never ease out at the join.

**Make it.** Forms 1 and 3 in Fusion on the cut: Transform scale keyframes on an accelerating curve,
motion blur on; the word is a Text+ title (C7), so no model draws it. Form 2 is generated as the
outgoing clip's tail: `kling3_0` first and last frame with the last frame a crop of the opening's dark
(`motion-design.md` §2.1, the crop method), or blocking `push_through` rendered as the video reference
for `minimax_h3` or `seedance_2_5`. The incoming clip's start frame opens on the same dark or colour.
It counts as one accent under the transition budget (C5).

**Prompt (form 2).** Outgoing, one camera behaviour: the place first, then "the camera moves forward
toward the dark open doorway, faster and faster, until the darkness fills the whole frame in the last
half second"; then "one continuous shot, no cut". Incoming: "opens on darkness that resolves as the
camera keeps moving forward into [the next place]".

**QA.** The scale rate still rising into the cut (no ease-out in the last 4 frames). Both sides of the
cut on the same dark or colour (mean luma within about 10 grey levels `[house]`). The opening is the
subject's own. Once per film.

**What it does.** The eye is already on the opening, so the next picture appears exactly where the
viewer is looking — a cut on a motion onset at a matching screen position (`motion-craft.md` §4, Smith
2012). It is "match on black" given a target, and on a generated clip it hides the incoming clip's
settle head inside a frame with nothing in it to settle.

**Breaks when.** The opening is not the subject's own: a random dark patch reads as a dip to black. The
dive eases out before the cut, so the join shows. The next shot opens on a different dark or colour.
It is used twice and becomes the film's grammar.

## 37. The punched still

*Provenance: Scenario's kinetic-film engine (`Picture` block, engine/blocks.js; references/craft.md),
SC 1.22, 2.10, 2.11 `[A·s; the rates computed here from the code]`; the failure it fixes is the authors'
`[A·m · their Kaiju teaser]`; the calm rates are the course's real-estate interiors, course notes `[C·m]`.*

**For.** Keep a still alive on screen without generating video: real product photography, a client's key
art, a Mode S still inside a cut.

**Spec.** A still that holds the screen for more than about 0.6 s gets a push, a drift and a slight turn
at once:
- **Calm register (any lane; the default):** a push of 3–6 %/s on a slow-in, slow-out, a drift of a few
  % of the width, a roll within ±1°. The course's interiors push at a median 6.4 %/s (range 1.4–25), and
  its notes propose a warning above about 10 %/s held for more than 2 s `[C·m · course notes]`.
- **Fast register (a type film, never the ads lane):** the picture 1.06 → 1.30 inside a camera push
  1.00 → 1.10 — ≈ ×1.35 net over 8 beats (2.8 s at 172 BPM), ≈ 12.5 %/s — with ±25 px of camera drift,
  70 px of image pan, a roll of −1° → +1° and a 3D turn of −3° → +3° `[A·s]`. Without the engine's
  punches, shakes and flashes: those wait in Appendix P (P7, P9).
- **Cut-ins:** hard cuts on a beat to a detail of the same still, each still growing, up to ×1.7; past
  ×1.7 a 2,048 px source shows softness at 4K `[A·s]`.
- **The slash open:** the picture opens along a diagonal over 0.45 s (ease-out) while settling from
  ×1.18 to ×1 `[A·s]` — without the engine's white flash.

**Make it.** In the edit, never generated: Resolve Inspector Zoom, Position and Rotation keyframes on the
still, or a Fusion Transform; each cut-in is its own timeline clip of the same still; the slash open is a
Fusion polygon mask animated along the diagonal. Darken the still for type with a graded tint.

**Prompt.** None for the move. For key art that will be punched: describe the composition as an art
director would — what sits in each third — and never ask for space for a title (the image model draws a
grey band, `[A·m]`).

**QA.** The push rate inside the register's band (3–6 %/s calm; over 10 %/s for more than 2 s only in a
type film). Cut-ins ≤ ×1.7, and every crop still at or above delivery resolution.

**What it does.** A held still is where a fast film parks. The authors' teaser failed its own dynamism
check on two pictures held 1.5 s and 1.2 s and passed, at 24 % still frames, once the block did this
`[A·m]`.

**Breaks when.** The fast register is used in a calm film — 12.5 %/s is twice the course's interiors. A
cut-in goes past ×1.7. Words are slammed over it with a letter tumble (C2: land them and stop).

## 38. The let-go crane

*Provenance: Scenario's previs-to-picture skill (references/pipeline.md, scripts/mix_to_shot.py),
one 15 s shot, SC 1.5, 1.6, 4.20 `[A·m · n = 1]`; the level curve reproduced in our plan
`[O·m · previs plan §6.5]`; blocking `let_go_crane` (2026-10-29).*

**For.** End a journey on the place, not on the vehicle or the walker.

**Spec.** Track alongside at a fixed distance — the authors' car: 6.5–7.8 m off, the camera 1.5 m high,
28 mm `[A·m]`. Then crane up (to 30 m) while easing the lens slightly wider (to 26 mm) and **slowing the
camera**, so the subject keeps its speed and pulls away. The aim blends from the subject to a fixed
heading for the reveal, clamped so the subject never leaves frame; a pan at the top uncovers the place.
One take, 15 s at 24 fps `[A·m]`. A 32 mm first take put the car outside the frame `[A·m]`. The
subject's sound follows the real distance: gain (7 m / d)^0.9, capped at 0 dB, so about −20 dB through
the crane (−19.3 dB at 90 m) `[O·m]`; sampled every 12 frames instead of every frame, a cut's level
change starts 0.5 s early `[O·m]`.

**Make it.** Blocking first: `"move": "let_go_crane"` rides alongside (`distance`, `height`, `side`),
releases at 45 % of the shot by default (`release`), slows to a stop on a smoothstep, rises to
`altitude`, eases the lens from `lens` to `to_lens`, and blends the aim toward a point `reveal_dist`
ahead, clamped to keep the subject inside 60 % of the half-frame `[house]`. The default altitude is 12 m
on the crane rig `[house]`; for the authors' 30 m give `"altitude": 30, "rig": "drone"` on a 12–15 s
shot. Or chain `side_track` → `crane_up` + `pan_reveal` with a blend (`blocking.md` §3a). Render the
blocking as the video reference for `minimax_h3` (Video 1) or `seedance_2_5` (`omni_reference`): a crane
is F as text (`cinematography-craft.md` §3.8). In Fairlight, automate the subject's level from the
blocking's camera distances, per frame (`blocking.py distances` is proposed, not built). If a composer
brief is written, name its sections after the move (drive, rise, reveal) with lengths, never clock
times (SC 4.21); the owner picks the music.

**Prompt (reference mode).** Five blocks, in this order (SC §10, the blockout pattern): the reference
(copy camera, framing, timing and motion from the video; the still is one moment in the final look,
never a framing); the stand-in (it only marks where the car is and how big); the action, beat by beat as
the render shows it — alongside, then the camera rises and falls behind while the road opens ahead —
never the story and never the move's name; the state (what is lit, what is far); the tail ("one
continuous take, no cuts").

**QA.** The subject in frame on every frame (blocking `check`). The camera's ground speed under a third
of the subject's by the end `[house]`, so the release reads. The subject's screen size shrinking steadily
after the release. The level about 20 dB down by the top of the crane.

**What it does.** The subject is released into the world it is crossing while it is still in frame —
what a crane rising off a scene means (`cinematography-craft.md` §3.4) — and the falling level sells the
distance the picture shows.

**Breaks when.** The lens is too long at the start: project the subject into the lens before rendering.
The camera keeps pace while it rises: no release, just a drone shot. The reveal arrives too late. The
path is prompted instead of supplied.

## 41. The locked-clock versions

*Provenance: Scenario's alter skill (scripts/alter_versions.py, alter_finish.py, references/qa.md;
MIT), 11 versions of one generated plate, SC 4.1–4.4, 2.17 `[A·m · n = 1 take]`; the room form from the

**For.** Show many versions of one take — wardrobe, colourway, world — without the performance jumping.

**Spec.** Every version comes from the same plate through an edit that keeps its motion, so all share

(0.5 s at 24 fps) on the plate's own clock, the plate's sound under all of them, the plate first if
wanted `[A·m]`. Trim the **same frames** from every version: six of eleven finals drifted 3–9 frames in
the last ⅓ s, so all were cut to frames 0–183 of 193 `[A·m]`. Drop a 0.5 s head; leave at least 0.5 s

**Make it.** Each version is a `seedance_2_5` `video_edit` of the one plate behind `gate.py`
(`motion-design.md` §2.4: a source of at least 4 s, the duration matching it, one edit stated). One
change per run; versions run in parallel on the original, never chained — two changes in one run drifted
more `[A·m]`. The reel is cut in Resolve: a cut every 12 frames on the plate's timecode, the plate's own
sound on A1.

**Prompt.** Opening: edit the video and keep everything frame for frame — the person described without
the thing that changes, the camera, framing, lens, set, light and timing. One change line. Close: nothing
else changes; no text, letters or logos. Never write "watermark": it is refused and still charged (scenario-common-patterns.md

**QA.** Every version's frame count equals the plate's after the shared trim. A sync grid of every
version at one frame (the authors used 3.0 s) shows the same pose. Tail drift checked on each version
before the trim is fixed.

**What it does.** The performance never jumps, so the eye reads only what changed — which is the whole
point of a variant, and the cheapest proof that one take carries many offers.

**Breaks when.** One version was regenerated on its own and is off the clock. The trims differ. The
change is a result claim: a before/after of a product working is a demonstration, so both sides must be
real (`real-and-ai.md` §5), and a wipe between a real and an edited side is not a deliverable. In Mode B
it is still one hero's variants, not a batch (alter plan C9). ⛔ Not for a recast of a real person (C5),
a product swap that carries a mark (C6), or a room in a property listing (W1). A target frame for a
version goes only through the gated `target:<SHOT>` opt-in (C1).

## 51. The struck operator

*Provenance: course notes, three plays of one staged fight `[C·m]`; an action variant of §33.*

**For.** Put the viewer inside a fight: they take the hit. ⛔ Drama and action only — never the ads lane
(`product-video.md` §2.5 bans camera shake there).

**Spec.** On each contact the frame is knocked 8–11 % of the width in the screen direction of the blow
and dips 2–20 % of the height, with a scale step of ±5–7 % and a roll of −8° to +4°; the blow itself is
1–2 motion-blurred frames (0.05–0.10 s); then a constant drift back at 5–20 %/s over 0.7–1.6 s. Contacts
0.3–1.6 s apart. Jitter 27–63, frantic `[C·m]`.

**Make it.** Generate the fight on a locked or gently following camera, then add the texture in post, in
three layers (course notes): a base random walk of 5–10 %/s with ±3° of roll; an impulse on each
contact frame (the knock above, in the blow's direction, with motion blur); nothing between contacts but
the base walk. Resolve Inspector Position, Zoom and Rotation keyframes, or Fusion; the contacts from
`action_points.py`. A ×0.84 scale step needs about 1.2× overscan: generate at 2K or 4K. Previs: a `react`
of `kind: jolt` — ⚠️ its sideways kick alternates by sample index today, not by the blow (course notes
item 5).

**Prompt.** If the jolt is generated: one per prompt, anchored to the contact, with its screen
direction — "As her fist lands, the camera is knocked with the blow, a sharp jerk to the left and down,
one blurred frame, then it drifts back over about a second." Expect a smooth path anyway and add the
texture in post.

**QA.** Every jitter peak within ±2 frames of a contact. Shaky, so a fail: over 65 sustained beyond about
1 s, or 25 or more held past about 2.5 s with no contact driving it `[C·m band]`.

**What it does.** The camera moves the way the punch travels, so the viewer feels hit. Peaks between
contacts read as a bad operator, not as intent.

**Breaks when.** The knock goes against the blow. The shake runs between contacts. It is used outside a
fight.

**Prompt.** "Top-down on hands typing at a keyboard. The camera tilts up and draws back, slowing, until
the whole desk and the room behind it are in view." Ease out over 4–6 s.

**QA.** The pull front-loaded (the first third at least twice the last); the place is the subject by the
end.

**What it does.** It shows what is being done and then where, the order a viewer needs for a process.

**Breaks when.** The two end frames disagree on the room. The hands stop before the pull ends, so the task
vanishes.

## 58. The vertical pivot

*Provenance: course notes `[C·m]`; blocking `vertical_pivot` (2026-10-29).*

**For.** Hold a subject still while the world pivots behind them; give a static subject life.

**Spec.** Frame A high, looking down; frame B low, looking slightly up, with a mid-ground layer entering at
the lower edge. The subject holds the same place and size: the box within ±3 % in height and ±0.04 of the
width for 5.3 s. The background shifts 13–56 % of the height, easing out from 3–8 %/s to 1–3 %/s. Lens
50–100 mm. Jitter 0.84–0.98 (float) `[C·m]`.

**Make it.** The course's most model-friendly move: the model only has to keep one thing still.
`kling3_0` first and last frame; `minimax_h3` start and end frames as the second arm. Previs:
`"move": "vertical_pivot"` — `pivot` 0.45 of the frame height, 70 mm, MCU, and `above` 0.6 of the pivot
spent above the eyes `[house]`.

**Prompt.** Frame A: "high angle, looking down at him, chest-up and centred, [the place] behind". Frame
B: "low angle, looking slightly up, the same size and place in frame, [sky and treetops] behind, [a
fence] entering at the bottom edge". Motion: "The camera sinks and moves forward while tilting up; he
stays exactly where he is in the frame while the background slides down behind him; slowing to a stop."

**QA.** Between the first and last frames: subject size within ±5 % and centre within ±0.05 of the width;
a background shift of at least 15 % of the height `[C·m band]`.

**What it does.** The subject is the fixed point of the shot, so the shot is about them even though
everything else moves.

**Breaks when.** The two frames change the subject's size or place: it reads as a push or a reframe. The
background has no depth to pivot.

## 59. The structure sweep

*Provenance: course notes `[C·m]`.*

**For.** Show a whole building or a stage.

**Spec.** A tilt down of 35–73 % of the height with a pan of 7–23 % of the width, about 2–3 : 1, over
2.5–6.7 s. Fast takes tilt 15–24 %/s and pan 6–11 %/s; the slow take tilts 6–7 → 4 %/s and pans
2–3 %/s, decelerating; from sky and treetops down to the house, the person walking in at the bottom
(0.24 → 0.46 of the height) `[C·m]`. The 2–5 °/s of roll whose sign follows the pan is geometry — the pan
rate × sin(pitch) — not a camera roll `[C·m]`.

**Make it.** Three behaviours at once (tilt, pan, a walk against the pan), so only as an endpoint pair:
`kling3_0` first and last frame. Blocking `tilt_reveal` with `from.look_at` on the sky and `to.look_at`
on the building: one move that tilts and pans together.

**Prompt.** Frame A: sky and treetops above the roofline. Frame B: the whole facade, [the person]
entering at the bottom. Motion: "The camera tilts down and turns slightly right, slowing, until the whole
house is in view; [the person] walks in at the bottom."

**QA.** Tilt to pan between 2 : 1 and 3 : 1, decelerating. Do not flag the geometric roll up to 5 °/s.

**What it does.** Scale arrives in the order the eye reads a building, top to bottom, and ends on the
person at its foot.

**Breaks when.** It is prompted as text: it collapses to a tilt or a pan. The ratio flips mid-move.

## 60. The roll layer

*Provenance: course notes and its edit recipes, item 6
`[C·m]`.*

**For.** A stylised flip of the world, or a flying bank. ⛔ Drama, 16:9. In 9:16 or an ad, keep any bank
to 2–5 °/s or leave it out (`cinematography-craft.md` §3.8, the roll row).

**Spec.** **Barrel roll:** the world rotates 60–75° in 1.6–1.7 s, or 249° in 5 s — 38–63 °/s sustained, or
accelerating 14 → 100–154 °/s on a short flip — while the subject stays near upright `[C·m]`. **Bank:**
2–5 °/s over flat surfaces; 18–31 °/s in the action, about 50° one way over 2–2.5 s and back `[C·m]`.

**Make it.** In the edit, never prompted. Generate a side-on flip with headroom on every side, then
Resolve Transform Rotation Angle keyframes at 40–60 °/s for a 5 s roll, or about 15 → 100–150 °/s for a
1.5 s flip, scaled up to hide the corners. For a bank: generate the flight level, then add a sine
rotation of ±3–5° over 4–5 s (calm) or ±25° over 5 s (drama).

**Prompt.** None for the roll. Ask for the level shot with room on every side.

**QA.** No corner shows through the rotation; the rate inside the bands above; the roll starts on an
action.

**What it does.** The world turns and the subject does not, which reads as their experience rather than
the camera's mistake.

**Breaks when.** It is used in 9:16 or in an ad. It is generated: the model warps the geometry.

## 61. The pinned slider parallax

*Provenance: course notes — the entry the course notes proposed as the
catalogue's first measured one `[C·m]`; the slow, short sibling of §32.*

**For.** Make a still performer feel alive, slowly: the subject holds while the world turns.

**Spec.** **Side to side:** the frame shifts 1.3–1.8 %/s for 3.4–6.2 s (6–8 % of the width), near
constant, while lit candles close to the lens cross at 4–5.6 %/s and the performer moves ≤ 1 %/s
`[C·m]`. **Parallax** (the slide plus a counter-pan): the subject drifts ≤ 2 % of the width; the
background moves 2–6 %/s one way and a near practical the other way; 4.6–18 s; jitter 0.61 on the clean
take `[C·m]`. Without a near layer the move is invisible.

**Make it.** Trucking is F as text (§3.8): supply first and last frames in which only the foreground layer
has moved, by 20–30 % of the width (`kling3_0`), or the blocking render as the video reference for
`minimax_h3` or `seedance_2_5` — `parallax_slide` with `keep: <subject>`, `distance` up to 1.0 m (a
42-inch slider travels 1.07 m) and a set piece near the lens. In an ad, use the side-to-side form; the
counter-pan form waits with the orbits there (Appendix P, P10).

**Prompt.** "The camera slides slowly to the right at a perfectly even speed, turning to keep his hands in
the same place in frame; the candles behind drift one way, the candle in front the other; he stays where
he is."

**QA.** Frame ≤ 2 %/s, constant within ±25 % across its thirds; the foreground ≥ 4 %/s and at least 3× the
subject's; subject drift ≤ 3 % of the width; shake outside the person box ≤ 0.8; roll ≤ 1°
`[C·m band]`. Measure outside the person box and the flicker: candles and playing hands made a steady
rig read 12.4 `[C·m]`.

**What it does.** The mechanism of §32 at the scale of a room: one thing pinned, the perspective wrapping
round it.

**Breaks when.** There is no near layer. The counter-pan lags and the subject slides. A bright practical
in the foreground takes the first fixation (`cinematography-craft.md` §4.2): it must repeat the scene's
own light and keep moving.

## 62. The lean

*Provenance: course notes `[C·m]`.*

**For.** A tight detail or an abstract, approached the way a person leans in.

**Spec.** The lens arcs forward over the subject's shoulder about a planted foot while the camera
counter-tilts to keep the detail placed. The detail grows ×1.36–1.40 over 4.8 s; the scale rate eases in
and out, 2.4 → 14 (the peak about halfway) → 6 %/s; the counter-tilt ±3.5 %/s; jitter about 2 (float).
The nearest layer slides out of the frame while the detail moves a tenth of the width: approach, not
zoom `[C·m]`.

**Make it.** Generated: one push with a small tilt riding on it, which stays one move type
(`realism-qa.md` V8) only while the tilt is ≤ 4 %/s. Or `kling3_0` first and last frame with the last a
crop of the first. Blocking: `push_in` with `dur` about 4.8 s — the plugin has no monopod rig yet (course notes
§15 item 1).

**Prompt.** "The camera leans in over his shoulder toward the tablet, about 40 % closer over five seconds,
tipping down to keep it in the same place; it eases in and settles."

**QA.** The detail ×1.3–1.45 over 4–5 s by its own size; easing in and out; jitter ≤ 2.5; a near layer
leaving or crossing the frame `[C·m band]`.

**What it does.** It reads as curiosity rather than a machine's push: the arc and the counter-tilt are
what a body does.

**Breaks when.** The tilt grows into a second move. The detail drifts in frame.

## 63. The ceiling exit

*Provenance: course notes, measured twice `[C·m]`; blocking `ceiling_exit` (2026-10-29).*

**For.** Leave a scene; a transition into the next.

**Spec.** The vehicle starts to leave, and within 0–1 s the frame tilts up off it to a neutral surface — a
concrete ceiling, the sky: 58–66 % of the height over 4.8–5.2 s, starting at 17–19 %/s and landing at
4–7 %/s, float (2.4–2.6) `[C·m]`. Cut on the empty surface, or match it to a tilt down into the next
scene.

**Make it.** Generated (T: one tilt per clip; in 9:16 the vertical is the native move). Previs:
`"move": "ceiling_exit"` — `frame_heights` 0.62, `dur` 5 s, 55 % of the travel in the first 30 % of the
time, a shape fitted to the measured rates `[house]`.

**Prompt.** "As the truck pulls away, the camera tilts up off it to the concrete ceiling, about two-thirds
of the frame height over five seconds, slowing to a stop."

**QA.** 35–66 % of the height over 4–5 s; a mean of 9–14 %/s; the first third at least twice the last;
jitter ≤ 2.7 `[C·m band]`.

**What it does.** A rise off a scene means release (`cinematography-craft.md` §3.4), and the empty surface
is a clean cut point that a generated take otherwise never gives.

**Breaks when.** The surface has something in it to read. The tilt starts before the subject moves (the
trigger rule, §3.8).

## 64. The locked timelapse

*Provenance: course notes `[C·m; the interval settings are PRACTITIONER · course notes]`.*

**For.** Time passing in one place; a reset beat between sequences.

**Spec.** The camera is locked — every ground band read 0.00 %/s on the course's runs — and one named
element moves: the sky, the traffic, a figure `[C·m]`. Interval bands: clouds in sun 2 s (about 2 %/s on
screen); overcast with wind 4 s (4–5.6 %/s); dusk with a person walking in 1 s (about 1 %/s, then sped
×4); night 4.2 s (exposures of 2–3 s); stars 30 s. The duty (shutter ÷ interval) under 1 % reads crisp
and staccato (his day runs, 0.2–0.4 %); 50–100 % reads smooth (his night, 76 %). Frames = run (min) × 60
÷ interval (s); clip length = frames ÷ 23.976; at least 300 frames (12.5 s). ND stops = log2(target
shutter ÷ metered shutter) — derived, not taught.

**Make it.** Real footage: import the numbered stills as one clip in Resolve and set Clip Attributes to
23.976 before anything else (a 300-frame run left at 30 plays 25 % fast); grade one frame and copy it to
the clip; spatial noise reduction, not temporal (our inference); ProRes 422 HQ; an exposure jump is split,
graded and dissolved, or ramped with a keyframed gain. Generated: one locked clip, any roster model.
⚠️ `assets/aerial-moves.json` maps `timelapse` to the drone hyperlapse; a ground timelapse is not that
move (course notes).

**Prompt.** "Time-lapse, locked off on a tripod; the camera is entirely motionless; the rock and the city
do not move; clouds drift right about a fifth of the frame width in ten seconds" (about 2 %/s, the 2 s
look). For the 4 s look: "clouds race across half the frame in ten seconds" (about 5 %/s). Give the
moving element a speed in frame terms, never "fast".

**QA.** Ground band ≤ 0.3 %/s; the sky constant in direction and within ±30 % in speed; shadows turning
one way; flicker under 0.5 % (0.002 in `look_qa` units); white balance fixed unless the light changes; a
person moving in jumps, not smoothly, at intervals over 1 s `[C·m band]`. Measure the camera on the
slowest band: a whole-frame tracker reads cloud as camera motion `[C·m]`.

**What it does.** The world moves at a rate a person cannot while the place stays still, so the place
becomes the subject.

**Breaks when.** Nothing in frame moves. The camera drifts — it reads as a mistake, not a move (use §65).
Speed words such as "racing" appear without the time-lapse.

## 65. The fake timelapse move

*Provenance: course notes `[C·m]`; the
slider's real motion timelapse, course notes `[C·m]`.*

**For.** Movement inside a timelapse without moving the camera.

**Spec.** Keyframed scale and position on a locked timelapse: in to a tight frame by the middle, then out
with a drift up. At normal playback about 1–1.4 %/s of push and 0.7 %/s of pull, with 0.3–0.9 %/s of
drift (the demo); the shipped Rome clips ran 1.4–6.5 %/s of scale (median ≈ 3) with 0.7–2 %/s of drift
`[C·m]`. Headroom from his stills: 2.85× in 1080, 1.42× in 4K, 1.9× for a 9:16 crop. The tell is that
there is no parallax — every band moves together, where a slider's bands differ by 5–6 %/s — so keep it
at or under about 3 %/s and always pair it with a drift. A real slider timelapse pushes 1.2–1.7 %/s with
real parallax and shake ≤ 0.65 `[C·m]`.

**Make it.** Resolve Inspector Zoom and Position keyframes, linear (a digital zoom runs 1–4 %/s, linear,
over 2–4 s: course notes). Generation-safe: the model renders only a locked shot.

**Prompt.** §64's locked prompt; the move is added in the edit.

**QA.** Scale rate ≤ 3 %/s and constant; a drift present; jitter no higher than the source's; the crop at
or above delivery resolution.

**What it does.** The eye gets a direction inside a shot that has none, at no generation cost.

**Breaks when.** It runs faster than about 3 %/s and the missing parallax shows. It eases: a post move
eased at that speed moves like nothing a camera does.

## 66. The walked hyperlapse

*Provenance: course notes `[C·m]`; the planning arithmetic is ours, from
the course's numbers.*

**For.** A city's bustle; arriving at a landmark.

**Spec.** Time compression with a pinned subject. **Landmark push:** one step per still, 24 mm; growth
×1.9–3.1 over 1.7–1.8 s — 47–90 %/s, 1.6–2.7 % per frame; drift ≤ 9 % of the width; jitter 4.1–5.3; step
CV 0.20–0.28; frame-to-frame luma flicker 0.5–0.8 % `[C·m]`. The best case, along leading lines: ×1.8 in
1.7 s (41 %/s), jitter 2.9, roll wobble 0.09° `[C·m]`. **Far pin, night city:** 26 stills, about 16 mm,
f/2.8, 1/125 s; growth 44–54 %/s; the pin at x 0.50, y 0.33 of the still, held within 1.2 % of the width
and 1.1 % of the height `[C·m]`. **Planning:** a push that grows by k over N stills at step d starts
D₀ = N·d·k / (k − 1) away — ×2.6–3.1 over 44 stills means about 65–70 steps (45–50 m at 0.7 m), and ×1.5
over 72 stills about 216 steps (150 m). To land the pin on the 16:9 upper third from a 3:2 still, place
it at y 0.36 of the viewfinder; for a 9:16 cut keep it within x 0.31–0.69.

**Make it.** Real footage: the stills as one image-sequence clip in Resolve (Clip Attributes 23.976
first); a light stabilise (Similarity, Zoom on, a low Smooth) or, on a rough set, a two-point track in
Fusion first; Deflicker for lit signs (check the control names on Studio 21.1). Generated: any roster
model with the time-lapse words, or `kling3_0` first and last frame with the pin on the same screen point
and the last frame a centre crop of the first (×2–3).

**Prompt.** "Time-lapse hyperlapse walking toward the clock tower, the clock locked at the top centre of
the frame in every frame while the street, trees and clouds stream past, a steady forward speed, the
building growing to about three times its size over two seconds." At night with a short shutter: the
people crisp but jumping position between frames, the screens changing image every frame. Always the
word time-lapse (as `aerial_qa.py` already requires of aerials), and generic, unbranded signage.

**QA.** Pin drift ≤ 2 % (flag over 3 %); step CV 0.15–0.5 (under 0.08 reads as a digital zoom; over 0.5,
uneven steps); jitter ≤ 5.5; flicker ≤ 1 % unless screens fill a third or more of the frame, then up to
about 8 %; the building's geometry rigid; the pin in frame to the last frame; no near object jumping
across `[C·m bands]`. Frozen billboard content across a generated hyperlapse is a tell.

**What it does.** The place moves at the speed of the city while the landmark holds still, so the arrival
feels inevitable.

**Breaks when.** Trees line the path: jumps of 13–23 % of the width that nothing stabilises. The walk
grows ×3 and pushes the landmark out of the top in the last third. An orbit looking up into the sky
(flicker 9.7 %, jitter 35–42). A repeating pattern — a corridor of doors — defeats the stabiliser.

## How this file grows — and the honest constraint on it

**I cannot watch video.** Every entry above comes from a written prompt plus a stated outcome; none
of it comes from me judging a frame. That is the binding limit, and it decides which sources are
actually usable:

| Source | Usable? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Shot-by-shot BREAKDOWNS of films and promos** | ⚡ **yes, and this is the best one** | a breakdown is somebody who *did* watch it writing down what the shot was and why it worked. It converts footage into exactly the form this file needs — a named move, its spec, its function. Prefer breakdowns that name the mechanics (lens, move, cut point, blocking) over ones that describe the feeling |
| **Production prompts with their revision history** | yes | what the corpus above is. The revisions are the valuable half: they say what failed |
| **Rejected takes with a stated reason** | ⚡ **yes, worth more than the keeps** | a kept take tells you one thing worked; a rejected one with a diagnosis tells you what class of thing fails, which generalises |
| **A finished ad I am handed as a video** | ⚡ **yes, as of 2026-09-30 — via `scripts/film_breakdown.py`** | I still cannot WATCH it. But cuts, shot lengths, cutting rhythm and camera moves can be MEASURED, and frames can be LOOKED AT. The tool detects every cut, solves each shot's move from the pixels (push/pull as scale, pan/tilt as translation, dutch as rotation), separates camera motion from subject motion, estimates shot size from a face, and lays out a numbered sheet plus an IN/MID/OUT strip per shot. What it cannot supply is meaning — the beat a shot carries is still read off those sheets by a person. What that person reads off a frame — text, names, prices, taglines, claims — stays in the local notes and never passes into a client's script, cards or this catalogue; a card that did not read is written down as unread, never completed |
| **Motion design, UI animation, a launch film — what moves INSIDE the shot** | ⚡ **yes, as of 2026-10-24 — via `scripts/motion_track.py`** | `film_breakdown.py` gives one move per shot; a motion-design reference lives below that. The tool takes the camera out frame by frame, finds every element that still changes, cuts it out (GrabCut) and tracks it through the shot (ECC), and names each animation: what moved, how far, when, for how long, its easing (the nearest of 26 standard curves, a fitted CSS cubic-bezier, a spring), overshoot and settle, how it arrives (edge, pop, fade, cut) and the stagger between elements. Verified on a synthetic film with known moves `[measured 2026-10-24 · selftest]`. On real footage read its overlay sheets first: touching elements merge, thin ones get position only, a 3D move with parallax is flagged rather than solved |
| **"This ad performed well"** | not on its own | without the shot-level detail it becomes a formula, which is the failure this file exists to correct |
