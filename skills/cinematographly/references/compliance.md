# Compliance — claims, endorsements, and AI disclosure

For any brand in a claims-sensitive category — **sleep, wellness, beauty, supplements, health,
finance** — and for **every** video that uses a synthetic presenter. This is the gate the
✅ Branding Check enforces on top of the client profile's own non-negotiables.

**Scope note.** This file is US-centric (FTC / FDA) plus the three big platforms' own rules, because
that is where most ecommerce short-form is served. It is a **production checklist, not legal
advice** — the brand's counsel owns the final call, and the client's approved-language sheet always
wins over anything here. Where this file and a client's compliance sheet disagree, **the client's
sheet wins and you flag the conflict.**

---

## 0. The three separate obligations (they are not one thing)

Most compliance failures come from satisfying one and assuming the others are covered. They are
independent:

| # | Obligation | Owner of the failure | What satisfies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Substantiation** — the claim is true and backed | The advertiser's evidence file | Competent and reliable scientific evidence, held *before* the claim runs |
| 2 | **Endorsement honesty** — the speaker is real, is disclosed as not, and any connection to the brand is on screen | The video itself | Material-connection disclosure **in both channels, from frame one** (§2b); "Dramatization — not an actual customer" for a synthetic presenter |
| 3 | **AI disclosure** — the platform is told the content is synthetic | The upload | The platform's own toggle, plus an on-screen label where required |

**Disclosing that the presenter is an actor does not fix an unsubstantiated claim.** Two different
requirements, two different fixes. A video can be perfectly disclosed and still be a violation.

---

## 1. The substantiation standard

- The bar is **"competent and reliable scientific evidence"**: work done and evaluated by qualified
  people using generally accepted methods.
- **Hierarchy:** randomized controlled trials are the most reliable. Observational/epidemiological
  work shows association, not causation. **Animal and in-vitro studies alone have only limited
  value.** **Consumer testimonials and surveys are never sufficient** to substantiate a claim — no
  matter how many of them there are.
- **You cannot cherry-pick.** A cited study has to be broadly consistent with the surrounding body
  of evidence.
- **Net impression governs, not literal wording.** A structure/function sentence can still convey a
  disease claim through the product name, the imagery, or the context around it. The regulator's own
  worked example: an immune supplement named "Cold Away", shown with cold-symptom imagery, with copy
  about "nose and throat health in winter" — every individual phrase is legal and the ad still makes
  an implied disease-prevention claim requiring drug-level evidence.
  **In video this bites harder than in copy,** because the imagery is half the message. A safe line
  read over a shot of someone sniffling in bed is not a safe ad.
- **Hedge words do not rescue a claim.** "May", "promising", "preliminary", "helps support" do not
  adequately convey a limitation on their own. If the honest disclosure cannot be made clearly,
  **modify the claim or drop it** — do not shrink the qualifier.

---

## 2. Endorsements, testimonials, and synthetic presenters

- The definition of "endorsement" expressly covers **fake reviews, virtual influencers, and social
  tags** — this is the hook that reaches AI presenters. There is **no separate AI-endorsement rule**;
  the existing endorsement guides and the consumer-reviews rule already apply. (Treat any claim of a
  brand-new "2026 FTC AI rule" with suspicion — as of this writing no such federal rulemaking is
  final.)
- **A fabricated testimonial is the sharpest risk in this whole file.** A rule already prohibits
  reviews and testimonials that misrepresent being from someone who does not exist — **AI-generated
  fake reviews are named explicitly** — with civil-penalty exposure. An AI presenter describing a
  product experience they never had, presented as a customer, is squarely inside it.
  **Therefore:** a synthetic presenter may voice a *brand message*. A synthetic presenter must not
  be presented as a *real customer reporting a real result* without an unmistakable dramatization
  disclosure — and even then, the underlying claim still needs §1 substantiation.
- **An endorser can't say what the advertiser couldn't say directly.** Putting a claim in a
  character's mouth does not launder it.
- **Atypical results:** if a result shown is better than typical, the ad must make clear what results
  are **generally expected**. A bare "results not typical" tag does **not** cure the deception — state
  the actual typical outcome, at comparable prominence to the dramatic one.
- **Disclosure placement, for short-form video specifically:** the standard is outcome-based —
  "easy to notice and read in the time your followers have to look at it," and *unavoidable*. There
  is **no published minimum number of seconds**; treat any number you see quoted as unverified.
  Practical consequences:
  - a disclosure in the caption/description alone is **very unlikely** to qualify;
  - a platform's built-in disclosure tool **might not be adequate on its own**;
  - mid-video or end-of-video placement is easier to miss and therefore weaker;
  - the working standard is **large superimposed on-screen text**, present from the first frames,
    high contrast, inside the safe zone.
- **House rule for this skill:** any synthetic-presenter video carries an on-screen disclosure from
  frame one, burnt in, in the caption safe zone, sized like a caption and not smaller. Do not rely on
  a platform toggle alone; do not rely on the description.

## 2b. Disclosure — who is speaking, and does the viewer know it is an ad

This section was missing and it is the one that decides the most common short-form job: **a hired
creator, filming themselves, saying they like the product.** The rules below are US federal and were
verified against primary text on 2026-09-07. The Endorsement Guides were last amended **2023-07-26**;
the Reviews Rule took effect **2024-10-21**; neither has been amended since, and no FTC rulemaking
touching either appeared in the Federal Register through September 2026.

### 2b.1 There are two separate questions, and a brand account answers only one

| | The question | Cured by the brand's own handle? |
|---|---|---|
| **Format** | Before I engage, do I know this is advertising? | **Usually yes** |
| **Endorsement** | Once I am watching, do I know the person speaking was paid / gifted / employed? | **No. Never.** |

The regulator's own worked example is exactly our fact pattern — a brand's video inserted into a
social feed, attributable to the brand by name:

> "Based on their customary use of the social media site, consumers ordinarily would be aware that
> they did not subscribe to a post by ZYX Paints… Thus, in this situation, a disclosure informing
> consumers of the video's commercial nature **before they view it is not necessary**.
> **However**, given the video's format… consumers who view the video would reasonably believe an
> independent home improvement show produced the video and that it reflects the impartial views of
> the show's host… To avoid deception, **a clear and prominent disclosure informing consumers of the
> video's commercial nature is necessary in the video itself.**"
> [PRIMARY · FTC, *Native Advertising: A Guide for Businesses* (Dec. 2015), Example 14 · 2026-09-07]

The mirror image, for the same file posted on the creator's own account:

> "before watching the video, consumers likely would not expect it to be advertising, given their
> experience with other videos she had posted. Thus, **the video's commercial nature should be
> clearly and prominently disclosed before consumers view it.**"
> [PRIMARY · same, Example 16 · 2026-09-07]

⛔ **A UGC video that opens *"okay so I've been sleeping on this for three months and…"* is an
endorsement by an apparent third party, on any account.** If that person was paid, gifted, employed,
or synthetic, it must be disclosed **inside the video** — the brand handle in the byline does not
cure it, and neither does a post-click page. The controlling principle:

> "advertisers are responsible for ensuring that native ads are identifiable as advertising **before
> consumers arrive at the main advertising page**… disclosures that subsequently inform consumers of
> a natively formatted ad's commercial nature after they have clicked on and arrived at another page
> will not cure any misleading impression… **even if the truth is subsequently made known to
> consumers.**" [PRIMARY · FTC Enforcement Policy Statement on Deceptively Formatted Advertisements
> (2015), at 13; Native Advertising Guide §II · 2026-09-07]

And the reason format alone can be the violation, which is the sentence most people miss:

> "Such misleadingly formatted advertisements are deceptive **even if the product claims communicated
> are truthful and non-misleading.**" [PRIMARY · EPS at 10 · 2026-09-07]

### 2b.2 What triggers a disclosure

> "Material connections can include **monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products
> (including products unrelated to the endorsed product) to an endorser, regardless of whether the
> advertiser requires an endorsement in return.** Material connections can also include other benefits
> to the endorser, such as **early access to a product or the possibility of being paid, of winning a
> prize, or of appearing on television or in other media promotions.**"
> [PRIMARY · 16 CFR §255.5(a) · 2026-09-07]

So: cash, free product, a discount, a product they did not even review, early access, a *chance* of
being paid, or the prospect of appearing in the brand's own ads. The threshold is a **significant
minority**: "A material connection needs to be disclosed when a significant minority of the audience
for an endorsement does not understand or expect the connection."

**The advertiser is liable even where the endorser is not** — §255.1(d) requires the advertiser to
(1) guide endorsers, (2) monitor compliance, and (3) remedy non-compliance, and states plainly that
doing so is **"not a safe harbor."** Our contract templates and creator briefs are part of obligation
(1); the delivery log is part of (2).

### 2b.3 What a disclosure has to look like in a 35–55 s vertical video

The standard, verbatim: **"difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily understandable by
ordinary consumers"**, and in an interactive medium **"the disclosure should be unavoidable"**
[PRIMARY · 16 CFR §255.0(f) · 2026-09-07].

⛔ **Modality matching: a talking-head UGC ad uses both channels, so the disclosure needs both.**
§255.0(f) requires the disclosure in at least the visual portion when the representation is visual,
at least the audible portion when it is audible, and **both** when the representation uses both — and
"A disclosure presented simultaneously in both the visual and audible portions of a communication is
more likely to be clear and conspicuous." A creator talking to camera while the product is in frame
is *both*. The FTC also states each single-channel failure directly: **"if you just include an audio
disclosure, users who watch videos without sound won't hear it,"** and **"some viewers may watch
without sound and others may not notice superimposed words."**

**Placement.** *"Viewers are more likely to miss a disclosure at the end of the video, especially
since some may not watch the whole thing. Having it at the beginning of the review would be better.
Having multiple disclosures during the video would be even better."* And where an endorsement sits
mid-video: *"the best place for the disclosure would be right before or at the beginning of the
actual endorsement."* [PRIMARY · *FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking* · 2026-09-07]

**Duration.** ⚠ **The FTC has never published a number.** The standard is "long enough for ordinary
consumers to notice, read, and comprehend." The only figure in any primary document is a *failing*
example — a disclosure that "appears for only five seconds" **in combination with** small white
low-contrast text competing with other superimposed text [PRIMARY · §255.0(g)(9)(iii)]. That is a
holistic failure, not a five-second floor. See the blacklist below.

### 2b.4 The failure list — verified, and shorter than people expect

| Placement / wording | Verdict | The FTC's own words |
|---|---|---|
| Caption or description **alone**, on a video | ❌ | TikTok specifically: *"The text description on TikTok is in small print, it doesn't stand out… A disclosure in the text description is thus **very unlikely to be clear and conspicuous**."* YouTube: *"You can, but that's not enough on its own."* |
| Behind a **"more"** link | ❌ | *"the disclosure is not unavoidable and thus is not clear and conspicuous"* (§255.0(g)(9)(ii)) — this is the *Teami* and *Warner Bros.* failure |
| In the **comments** | ❌ | *"A disclosure in the comments to a post is easily avoidable and thus not clear and conspicuous."* |
| On the **profile / bio / About page** | ❌ | *"Listing your employer on your profile page isn't enough."* §255.0(g)(9)(i) |
| **Platform "Paid partnership" toggle alone** | ❌ | *"the ultimate responsibility for clearly and conspicuously disclosing a material connection rests with the influencer and the brand – not the platform. To be on the safe side, it's always best to add your own disclosure even if a platform offers its own disclosure tool."* §255.0(g)(9)(iii) gives the failing example |
| **Tagging the brand** | ❌ | *"Tagging a brand is an endorsement, but it's not a disclosure that you have a connection to a brand."* |
| Mixed into a **block of hashtags** | ❌ | *"Don't mix your disclosure into a group of hashtags or links."* |
| **#ad, "Ad:", "ad"** at the START | ✅ | *"'#ad,' 'Ad:,' and 'ad' at the beginning of a post are probably all equal"* — and it works for gifted product as well as paid |

**What passes, verbatim from the FTC's own list:** *"This is an ad for BRAND" · "This video is paid
for by BRAND" · "BRAND paid me to tell you about it" · "Thanks to Acme brand for the free product" ·
"Paid post by [brand]" · "AcmePartner" / "Acme Ambassador" / "#XYZ_Ambassador."*
[PRIMARY · FTC FAQ + *Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers* (Nov. 2019) · 2026-09-07]

### 2b.5 Insiders — and this one carries civil penalties

The Endorsement Guides are **interpretations**, not a rule: non-compliance is evidence supporting a
§5 charge, not itself a violation, and a plain §5 deception case carries **no civil-penalty
authority**. **16 CFR Part 465 is a rule**, and it does. Keep the two apart — the distinction decides
whether money is on the table.

> **§465.5(b)(1)** — it is an unfair or deceptive act "for **a business to disseminate or cause the
> dissemination of a consumer testimonial** about the business or one of the products or services it
> sells **by one of its officers, managers, employees, or agents**, which fails to have a clear and
> conspicuous disclosure of the testimonialist's material relationship to the business, when the
> relationship is not otherwise clear to the audience and the business knew or should have known…"
> [PRIMARY · 16 CFR §465.5 · 2026-09-07]

⛔ **Putting anyone who works for the brand on camera as a satisfied user is a civil-penalty
exposure, not a style choice.** The FTC's "otherwise clear to the audience" examples are narrow —
"an image or video showing the testimonialist **working in the company's offices or wearing a company
uniform**." An adequate disclosure can be as light as the testimonialist calling it **"my company's"**
product. The founder is covered too: §255.5(b)(4) makes an ownership stake specifically disclosable
and notes consumers are *more* likely to assume independence on social media than in a TV ad. The
enforcement precedent is *CSGO Lotto* (2017) — owners on camera as apparently impartial users.

**Other Part 465 prohibitions that reach a video pipeline:**

- **§465.2** — writing, creating, selling, purchasing or disseminating a review/testimonial that
  misrepresents that the reviewer **exists**, **used the product**, or **their experience** of it.
- **§465.4** — providing compensation "in exchange for, or conditioned expressly or by implication
  on, the writing or creation of consumer reviews **expressing a particular sentiment**." Incentives
  are legal; *conditioning them on positivity* is not, and "Tell us how much you loved…" is named as
  an implied condition.
- **§465.6** — a brand-controlled site or entity misrepresented as providing **independent** reviews.
  This is the "best mattress of 2026" comparison site and the invented "sleep institute" seal.
- **§465.7** — suppressing reviews by unfounded legal threats, or misrepresenting that displayed
  reviews are most or all of them.
- **§465.8** — buying or selling fake followers, views or likes "knew or should have known to be fake."

⛔ **The scripted-creator trap.** The FTC's own red-flag example: *"if someone gives a testimonial and
then asks for the product, a business should question whether the testimonialist used the product,"*
and *"a business shouldn't provide the text for a testimonial without a reasonable basis to conclude
that the text is truthful about the testimonialist's experience."* Handing a creator a script that
says **"I've slept on this for three months"** when they received it last week is squarely inside
§465.2(b). **Write experience claims to what the creator can actually attest, or write them out.**

**AI presenters are not banned** — the FTC says so directly: *"The rule has no blanket prohibition on
the use of AI-generated avatars in marketing… Section 465.2 of the rule is drafted specifically so as
to **not** prohibit companies from using virtual influencers."* The exposure is elsewhere: §5
deception if the avatar is presented as a real customer with a real experience, the rule if the
*underlying* testimonial is fake, and §465.2 for a celebrity likeness used without permission. Same
for human actors: *"Having actors portray testimonialists is not covered by the rule unless the
underlying testimonials were fake or false, but using actors to portray testimonialists could be
deceptive in violation of the FTC Act."* [PRIMARY · FTC, *Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule:
Questions and Answers*, updated 2025-05-01 · 2026-09-07]

**Civil penalty exposure: up to $53,088 per violation** — the FTC's own figure in its 2025-12-22
warning letters. What counts as *one* violation is undefined in the rule and untested. [PRIMARY ·
ftc.gov 2025 Fake Review Warning Template · 2026-09-07] · [UNVERIFIED — the per-violation unit]

### 2b.6 Every cut, every repost

⛔ **A disclosure does not survive a repurpose, and one per campaign is not enough.**

> ".com Disclosures: **'Use disclosures in each ad'**… 'the disclosure should be in **each and every
> ad** that would require a disclosure if that ad were viewed in isolation.'"
> [PRIMARY · FTC FAQ quoting .com Disclosures · 2026-09-07]

> §255.5(b)(3)(iii): where the original post lacked a clear and conspicuous disclosure, **or had one
> that does not appear clearly and conspicuously in the repost**, the brand "should clearly and
> conspicuously disclose its relationship… in its repost."
> [PRIMARY · 16 CFR §255.5(b)(3)(iii) · 2026-09-07]

This is the **whitelisting / Spark Ads / partnership-ad** failure: the creator's caption disclosure is
replaced by ad copy, or the burned-in text is cropped by a different aspect ratio, and the repost
ships undisclosed. It is also the *Warner Bros.* aggravating fact — the disclosure vanished entirely
when the videos were shared onward. **Route every repurpose through this file, not just the original**
(`modes/repurpose.md`), and re-check the disclosure **after** the reframe, in the delivered aspect.

### 2b.7 Platform mechanics — required, and never sufficient

| Platform | What it requires | Its own disclaimer |
|---|---|---|
| **TikTok** (Branded Content Policy published 2026-08-04, **effective 2026-08-31** — most summaries online describe the 2023 version) | Enable the **commercial content disclosure toggle** for anything gifted, paid, commissioned, or from an ongoing brand relationship. Separately: *"You must ensure that the product or service you are promoting is sufficiently clear, without requiring viewers to access your profile page or any links… explicitly identify the product or service verbally and/or in the text caption."* Toggle settings: "Your brand" → **Promotional content**; "Branded content" → **Paid partnership** | *"You are responsible for ensuring that any Branded Content that you post complies with all applicable laws and regulations… they are not exhaustive"*; restricted industries need *"any required disclosures and disclaimers that are **in addition to** Branded Content disclosures"* |

Interstitial cards outside of a video's first three seconds… must not persist for longer than three consecutive seconds."* Restricted (age-gate 18+): weight loss, cosmetic procedures, **subscription / negative-option / free-to-pay-conversion billing** — which a mattress trial-to-pay offer can trigger | *"You must also comply with all applicable laws and regulations, including by ensuring that you provide all necessary disclosures."* ⚠ No Meta statement was found saying the label is legally insufficient on its own — [UNVERIFIED] |
| **YouTube** | Tick **"My video contains paid promotion like a product placement, sponsorship, or endorsement."** YouTube then shows an automatic disclosure **for 10 seconds at the start**. Policy scope explicitly includes **Shorts** | Says it three times, e.g. *"different jurisdictions have various requirements… that may require you to do more. Be sure to check and follow applicable laws."* ⚠ Whether the automatic 10-second disclosure renders on a **Short** is not documented — [UNVERIFIED], do not rely on it |

**[PRIMARY · tiktok.com/legal/page/global/bc-policy/en; facebook.com/policies/brandedcontent;
support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235 · all 2026-09-07]**

⛔ **Meta's three-second title-card rule and the FTC's "disclose at the beginning" guidance point in
opposite directions.** Resolve it the way both allow: the disclosure is **not a title card** — it is
burned-in caption-layer text running *over* the opening action, present from frame one, in the safe
zone. That satisfies "before they receive the advertising message," survives Meta's card rule because
it is not an interstitial, and matches this file's existing house rule for synthetic presenters.

### 2b.8 House rules

1. **Both channels, from frame one.** Burned-in on-screen text **and** the spoken VO's first line,
   for every video with a material connection. Not a card. Not the post caption. Not the toggle
   alone. ⛔ **Placement: top of frame, inside the STRICT safe box** — 15% top / 35% bottom / 11%
   left / 28% right at 9:16, measured from TikTok's and Google's own template files
   (`delivery-format.md` §3). The bottom third is the platforms' own caption-and-CTA furniture, so a
   disclosure placed there is a disclosure the viewer never sees — which is the failure the FTC
   describes as "a disclosure in the lower corner of a video could be too easy for users to
   overlook."
2. **Plain words only:** "Paid partnership with [brand]" · "[Brand] paid me to make this" · "Ad."
   Never a hashtag block, never an abbreviation, never a logo.
3. **Set the platform toggle as well** — it is contractually required and it is not the disclosure.
4. **Re-verify after every reframe and every repurpose**, in the delivered aspect ratio, against the
   platform's UI chrome. ⚠ No FTC guidance exists on whether a disclosure must survive platform
   chrome; the adjacent language ("a disclosure in the lower corner of a video could be too easy for
   users to overlook", "a disclosure that is obscured by ads isn't clear and conspicuous") supports a
   top-or-centre placement rule but is not on point — [UNVERIFIED].
5. **Anyone connected to the brand says so on camera** — "my company's" is enough, and it is cheap.
6. **Experience claims match what the speaker can attest.** Duration of use, results, "I switched
   from" — all of it. If the creator got the product last week, the script does not say three months.
7. ⛔ **Answer the question in the lint, every script.** `claims_lint.py` takes `--paid` (someone here
   is paid, gifted, commissioned or employed — the normal UGC case) or `--no-material-connection`
   (nobody is). Whether a creator was paid is not inferable from a script's words, so a run that was
   never told reports **NOT ASSERTED** and is evidence of nothing. Record which flag was used.

⚠ **Scope.** US federal only. State UDAP statutes, the NY synthetic-performer statute and the EU AI
Act are covered in `real-and-ai.md` §5; the client's own approved-language sheet still wins.

## 3. Supplements: the structure/function line

- **Structure/function** = an effect on normal body structure or function ("calcium builds strong
  bones"). Allowed for supplements.
- **Disease claim** = diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent a disease. **Drugs only.**
- The required disclaimer, verbatim, where structure/function claims are made on a supplement:
  > This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not
  > intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.
- A 2025 relaxation concerns only how often the disclaimer must repeat **on a label**. It does not
  touch advertising: video disclosure still runs on the clear-and-conspicuous standard above.
- **The trap in video:** insomnia, anxiety, depression, and ADHD are **diagnosable conditions**.
  Naming any of them as something the product addresses converts a wellness ad into a drug claim.
- **Liver claims are the same trap** (researched 2026-10-21 for a liver-support supplement, lab-00009 R1):
  - Fatty liver (NAFLD/MASLD, and NASH/MASH) is a disease. FDA approved a drug for it in 2024 and NIDDK
    calls it a liver disease, so saying the product reduces liver fat in it, or treats it, is a disease
    claim [PRIMARY · 21 CFR §101.93(g); FDA press release, 14 Mar 2024 · checked 2026-10-21].
  - Lowering raised ALT/AST is an **implied** disease claim: FDA counts claims about a disease's
    characteristic signs ("reduces cholesterol" is its example), and NIDDK names raised ALT/AST as the
    sign of NAFLD. No FDA text names ALT/AST, so this is an inference. The safe form is "helps maintain
    liver enzyme levels already within the normal range", with the disclaimer above.
  - "Scar tissue" and "liver inflammation" are disease language too (damage to an organ).
  - **Pictures count.** "A picture of an abnormal tissue or organ would be an implied disease claim"
    [PRIMARY · FDA Small Entity Compliance Guide on Structure/Function Claims, 2002 · checked 2026-10-21].
    A clogged, glowing-red 3D liver is a picture of an abnormal organ, so the anatomy shot carries the
    same claim as the line it illustrates.
  - The enforcement precedent: FTC v. BASF / DIEM Labs (Hepaxa), 2021, a supplement sold as "clinically
    proven to reduce liver fat" whose own trial found it no better than placebo [PRIMARY · FTC press
    release, 1 Apr 2021 · checked 2026-10-21].
  - Substantiation is human RCTs on the formula or an essentially equivalent one at the same dose; animal
    and cell studies are background only [PRIMARY · FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance, Dec 2022 ·
    checked 2026-10-21]. Check every mechanism line against that before it is written: of four ingredient
    mechanisms checked for one liver product, none had human evidence (lab-00009 R1 Q7).

## 4. The claims lint — risky phrase → safe reframe

Run this over every script before it goes to generation. `scripts/claims_lint.py` automates the
mechanical pass; this table is the reasoning behind it and the source of the reframes. **The reframe
column keeps the selling power — the fix is precision, not timidity.** The client's own list sits on
top of this table: `claims_lint.py` reads the profile's "Restricted words for this brand"
(`clients/_TEMPLATE.md` §4), fails any use in the script, the captions and review quotes, and passes
only the replacement the client agreed — not a synonym the writer picks.

### Absolute / cure language

| Risky | Why | Safe reframe |
|---|---|---|
| "cures insomnia" | Disease claim | "many people say they fall asleep faster" |
| "treats anxiety" / "eliminates anxiety" | Disease claim; absolute | "designed to support a sense of calm before bed" |
| "cures / treats depression" | Disease claim; aggravated if aimed at parents or children | Do not make it. Point to a healthcare professional |
| "fixes your sleep problem for good" | Permanence + implied disease fix | "part of a consistent bedtime routine a lot of people stick with" |
| "no more sleepless nights" | Implies curing a diagnosable disorder | "supports a more restful night" |
| "reverses aging" | Condition-reversal framing | "supports skin's natural renewal" |
| "erases wrinkles" | Absolute cosmetic efficacy | "visibly softens the look of fine lines" |
| "permanent results" | Overstates durability | "results vary; continued use helps maintain them" |
| "works for everyone" | Universalizes a variable outcome | "a lot of people find it helps" |
| "detoxifies" / "flushes out toxins" | Unsubstantiable red-flag term | Describe the actual studied ingredient function |
| "boosts your immune system" (+ cold/flu imagery) | The canonical implied-disease example | "supports normal immune function" — **and strip the seasonal-illness imagery** |

### Evidence and authority

| Risky | Why | Safe reframe |
|---|---|---|
| "clinically proven" (unqualified) | Implies rigor not shown | Name the study: "in a 60-person double-blind trial, 72% reported…" — or delete the phrase |
| "clinically tested" (no specifics) | Same | Same fix |
| "scientists agree" / "doctors agree" | Overstates consensus | "supported by emerging research" — only if true |
| "doctor recommended" (no real doctor) | Unverifiable expert endorsement | "formulated with [named, credentialed, disclosed person]" — only if true |
| "#1 doctor recommended" | Unverifiable superlative | Drop unless a real, methodologically disclosed survey backs it |
| "backed by Harvard / Ivy League research" | Implied institutional endorsement | Cite the actual named study, or drop |
| "FDA approved" (supplement) | False — supplements are not FDA approved | "made in an FDA-registered facility" — only if true |
| "lowers cortisol" / "balances hormones" / "raises serotonin" | Specific biochemical mechanism without a human RCT — a live enforcement fact pattern | Drop the mechanism; keep subjective structure/function language |

### Outcomes, speed, and safety

| Risky | Why | Safe reframe |
|---|---|---|
| "lose 20 lbs in 2 weeks" | Atypical result as expectation | State the real average result, prominently, next to the claim |
| "melts fat while you sleep" | Weight-loss mechanism claim | "supports a healthy metabolism as part of your nighttime routine" |
| "results overnight" / "instant results" | Implausible speed | "many people notice a difference in the first week" — if substantiated |
| "guaranteed results" | Efficacy guarantee | "backed by a 30-day money-back guarantee" (satisfaction, not efficacy) |
| "no side effects" | Absolute safety claim | Omit, unless real adverse-event data supports it |
| "100% natural" used to mean safe | Natural ≠ safe | "made with natural ingredients. Check with your doctor if you're pregnant, nursing, or taking medication" |
| "safe to take every night, no need to ask your doctor" | Removes the standard guardrail | Always: "talk to your doctor before starting a new supplement" |
| "grow taller" (children's products) | Live enforcement fact pattern; minors involved | "supports healthy bone development" — structure/function only |
| "get slim legs right away" | Named as prohibited in platform ad policy | Gradual, substantiated, structure/function framing |

### Health ads: personal attributes, liver and weight claims (2026-10-21)

| Risky | Why | Safe reframe |
|---|---|---|
| "Do you have diabetes?" / "Struggling with fatty liver?" | Meta personal attributes: a question about the viewer's health is the policy's own rejected example (§5c) | Describe the product and what it is formulated to do |
| "Your sluggish liver is why you're tired" | Asserts the viewer's condition (Meta personal attributes) and names a disease sign | A named storyteller's experience, or the product's normal-function role |
| "If you're over 45…" / "Meet other women over 50" | Age is a personal attribute: "Meet other seniors" is a rejected example | Target the age in the ad set, not in the copy |
| "Clears the fat from your liver" | Disease claim; the BASF/Hepaxa claim | "Supports normal liver function", only with substantiation |
| "Lowers your ALT and AST" | Implied disease claim (§3) | "Helps maintain liver enzyme levels already within the normal range" |
| "Works without diet or exercise" | Not allowed by TikTok's Weight Management policy; an FTC red flag | "Alongside a balanced diet and regular activity" |
| A before-and-after transformation | Banned by TikTok as a product-effect comparison; Meta allows the impact of use with the time it took | Show use, with a substantiated timeframe |
| A close-up pinching belly fat | Banned depiction in Meta weight-loss ads | Show the person, not the body part |

`scripts/claims_lint.py` checks the wording in this table (personal attributes in every category; the
liver and weight rows with `--category supplements`). The pictures are a human read.

### Endorsement and review integrity

| Risky | Why | Safe reframe |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic presenter delivering a testimonial, undisclosed | Endorsement + fake-review exposure | On-screen from frame one: "Dramatization. Not an actual customer." |
| Fabricated star rating or review count on screen | Directly prohibited | Show only real, current, sourced aggregate data |
| Fabricated "as seen on [outlet]" | False media endorsement | Only real, verifiable placements |
| Bare "results not typical" under a dramatic before/after | Explicitly does not cure the deception | State what IS typical, with data, at equal prominence |
| Before/after showing wrinkles disappearing | Named as prohibited in platform ad policy | "visibly softens the look of fine lines over [substantiated timeframe]", real undoctored footage |
| "Paid actor" disclosed but the health claim still unsupported | Fixes obligation 2, not obligation 1 | Disclose **and** substantiate — see §0 |
| "#ad" buried at the end of a hashtag block | *"Don't mix your disclosure into a group of hashtags or links"* | "Ad" on screen from frame one **and** spoken; hashtags are not the disclosure |
| Disclosure only in the caption / description of a video | *"very unlikely to be clear and conspicuous"* (TikTok); *"not enough on its own"* (YouTube) | Burned-in caption-layer text from frame one, in the safe zone, plus the VO line |
| Relying on the Paid-partnership toggle alone | Responsibility *"rests with the influencer and the brand – not the platform"* | Set the toggle **and** disclose in the video |
| "Sponsored by [brand]" as the ad label | May read as *underwrote but did not create* — wrong for a brand-produced ad | "Ad" / "Advertisement" / "Paid partnership with [brand]" |
| An employee or founder on camera as a satisfied user, undisclosed | 16 CFR §465.5(b)(1) — **civil penalties**, unlike the Guides | The speaker says "my company's"; or disclose the role on screen |
| Script says "I've used this for three months" to a creator who got it last week | §465.2(b) — misrepresenting the testimonialist's experience | Write the claim to what the speaker can attest, or cut it |
| Reposting a creator's video to the brand account, disclosure cropped out | §255.5(b)(3)(iii) — the repost does not inherit it | Re-verify the disclosure **after** the reframe, in the delivered aspect |
| "Best mattress 2026" comparison page run by the brand, presented as independent | §465.6 — company-controlled review entity | Label the ownership, or do not present it as independent |

**Category note — finance.** The same three-obligation structure applies with different words:
performance claims need substantiation, testimonials need material-connection disclosure, and
"guaranteed returns", "risk-free", and specific-return figures are the equivalents of the cure
language above. Get the brand's approved language sheet before writing a finance script at all.

## 5. AI disclosure by platform

| Platform | Rule | Mechanism | Consequence of not doing it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TikTok** | Must label realistic images/audio/video completely generated or significantly edited by AI | Self-labelling tool (label / sticker / caption); auto-detection via C2PA Content Credentials also applies labels. **Ads:** the Ads Manager toggle "This ad contains AI-generated content" (since July 2024; non-Spark ads; it resets when a campaign is duplicated), or the advertiser's own caption, watermark or sticker [PRIMARY · TikTok Ads Policy and Business Help · checked 2026-10-21] | **Undisclosed synthetic content is ineligible for the For You feed** — a hard distribution penalty. An ad with undisclosed AI content "will be rejected or restricted". A "21 July 2026" start date circulates in blogs; no TikTok page carries it |
| **Instagram / Meta** | "AI Info" label applied on detection of industry-standard AI indicators, or on user disclosure. Approach is label, not remove. A voluntary account-level "AI Creator" label also exists. **Ads:** a commercial ad needs no advertiser disclosure; Meta labels it on detection, and offers optional self-disclosure in the European Region, California, New York, India and Taiwan. Disclosure is mandatory only for social-issue, electoral and political ads [PRIMARY · Meta Business Help Center, "AI info on ads" · checked 2026-10-21] | Upload disclosure + metadata detection | No confirmed reach penalty tied to the label itself; the risk is ad rejection and the general demotion of "unoriginal and spammy" content |
| **YouTube** | Must disclose when AI meaningfully alters or generates photorealistic content — real people appearing to say/do things, altered real footage, realistic scenes that didn't occur | "Altered or synthetic content" toggle in Studio at upload; auto-labelling from C2PA or its own detection | Repeated non-disclosure → manually applied label, up to removal or Partner Program suspension. **Disclosing does not itself limit reach or monetization** — YouTube says so directly |

**Music and singing voices (v2.40, checked 2026-10-27).** `composer.py elements <job>` writes the element
sheet, `09-qa/mix/ELEMENTS.md`, and `approve export` refuses without a current one once anything is composed
or generated.

- **TikTok:** the ads help page covers audio "completely AI-generated or significantly modified by AI".
  Switch on the toggle on every campaign and every duplicate (a duplicate resets it) [PRIMARY · TikTok
  Business Help].
- **YouTube:** disclosure is required for "AI generated music" that is the main focus of a video. An upload
  carrying C2PA metadata is labelled automatically, and the creator cannot remove the label. Tell the client
  before upload [PRIMARY · YouTube Help].
- **EU, Article 50(4):** has applied since 2 August 2026. A generic instrumental is unlikely to be a deepfake.
  A realistic AI singing voice, or an imitation of a real singer, likely is: AI vocals are disclosed, and
  soundalikes are never made [SECONDARY · law-firm briefings on the Commission guidelines of 20 Jul 2026].
- **Consent:** UK agency principles (IPA/ISBA Principle 11) ask for the client's agreement before any AI
  content is included, so consent is recorded before generation (`composer.py consent`).
- **Route-1 music** (composed here, rendered from licensed samples) is disclosed to the client on the element
  sheet. Whether a platform toggle applies is untested and goes to counsel; until then the owner decides per
  campaign (`route1_platform_label` in the composer's brief).

⛔ **Hybrid real+AI work raises exposure rather than lowering it, and the biggest risk is not
disclosure at all.** Under the product-demonstration doctrine (*FTC v. Colgate-Palmolive*, 380 U.S.
374, 1965) the test is whether a synthetic element carries the **proof of a product claim** — a
generated environment behind a real product is decoration, a generated shot of the product
*performing* is a mock-up standing in for a demonstration, and ⛔ **disclosing "AI-generated" may not
cure it**, because the deception is about the claim being unproven, not the technique being hidden.
**House rule: the proof shot is shot.** Full analysis, plus the platform triggers, the New York
synthetic-performer statute (in force 9 June 2026) and EU AI Act Art. 50 (applicable 2 August 2026):
`real-and-ai.md` §5.

**Exempt on YouTube** (and a useful general guide): non-realistic/animated content, beauty filters,
colour and lighting adjustments, cloning **your own** voice, captions, audio repair, script
assistance. **A stylized mascot is not the same risk surface as a photoreal synthetic human** — the
disclosure burden scales with photorealism.

**Hard rules regardless of platform:**
- **Never depict a private individual synthetically.** Banned outright on TikTok, disclosure or not.
- **Never put a real public figure in a synthetic commercial endorsement.**
- **Never clone a voice you don't have rights to.** Voice-likeness statutes exist and are being
  enforced at state level.
- **Synthetic-performer disclosure is becoming statutory, not just platform policy** — at least one
  US state now requires conspicuous disclosure of synthetic performers in advertising, with
  per-violation penalties, and the trend is toward more. Budget for the disclosure on every
  synthetic-presenter ad regardless of where it is served, because the audience is national.

**Above the platforms: the statutory layer, which is where an EU-served account actually lives.**
Platform policy is the fastest-moving obligation, not the highest one. ⛔ **`real-and-ai.md` §5 owns
this** — the EU AI Act's Article and application date, the New York synthetic-performer statute, and
the C2PA provenance standard the detection column above runs on. Two consequences worth carrying
here: the disclosure obligation **does not disappear because the platform's labelling rule is
satisfied**, and provenance signals travel with the file, so an undisclosed synthetic asset can be
labelled by a platform *after* it ships, by metadata rather than by review. The house rule is
unchanged and already stricter than the floor: disclose on frame one, burnt in.

## 5b. Blacklist — repeated in every agency deck, and wrong

Each of these is contradicted by, or unsupported in, the primary sources. Verified 2026-09-07.

1. **"The FTC requires the on-screen disclosure to be visible for at least 3 seconds"** (also quoted
   as 2, 5 or 7). **There is no primary source and the FTC has never set a duration.** The standard
   is "long enough for ordinary consumers to notice, read, and comprehend." The "3 seconds" figure
   most likely leaked in from **Meta's title-card format rule** — which is a *prohibition* on cards
   in the first three seconds, about sponsor branding, not disclosures. "10 seconds" is **YouTube's**
   automatic disclosure duration, not a rule. [UNVERIFIED — no FTC numeric minimum found]
2. **"The Paid-partnership toggle satisfies the FTC."** Contradicted three separate times in primary
   text (§2b.4), and disclaimed by TikTok and YouTube in their own policies.
3. **"The FTC banned #ad"** — no. *"'#ad,' 'Ad:,' and 'ad' at the beginning of a post are probably
   all equal,"* and it covers gifted product as well as paid. The failure is **placement**, not the
   word.
4. **"You only have to disclose if money changed hands."** Free or discounted product, unrelated
   product, early access, a prize chance, and the possibility of appearing in the brand's own media
   are all named in §255.5(a).
5. **"Naming the brand in the bio / tagging the brand is the disclosure."** Both explicitly rejected.
6. **"The Endorsement Guides are law."** They are "administrative interpretations" (§255.0(a)); a
   plain §5 deception case carries **no civil-penalty authority** at all. **Part 465 is a rule and
   does.** The distinction decides whether there is money on the table — do not blur it to sound
   serious.
7. **"§465.3 prohibits review hijacking."** **§465.3 is [Reserved]** — the Commission dropped the
   proposed provision. Review hijacking remains chargeable under §5, not under Part 465.
8. **"Incentivised reviews are illegal now."** Only if the incentive is **conditioned on sentiment**,
   expressly or by implication (§465.4).
9. **"AI-generated UGC actors are banned by the Reviews Rule."** The FTC says the opposite in terms
   (§2b.5). The exposure is deception about a real customer's real experience, not the technique.
10. **"One disclosure covers the campaign."** *"the disclosure should be in each and every ad that
    would require a disclosure if that ad were viewed in isolation."*
11. ⚠ **"There was a big FTC influencer-disclosure enforcement action last year."** No FTC action
    between the November 2023 aspartame warning letters and September 2026 turns on video disclosure
    *placement*; the recent docket is fake and incentivised reviews. If someone cites one, ask for
    the file number. [UNVERIFIED — searched the FTC case list; absence of evidence]

## 5c. Platform ad policies for health, weight and supplement ads (Meta and TikTok, US)

Checked 2026-10-21 against the platforms' own pages for a liver-support supplement aimed at a 45+ audience
(lab-00009 R1). These sit on top of §1–§3: a platform can reject an ad the FTC would allow.

**Meta — personal attributes.** An ad must not assert or imply the viewer's personal attributes,
including physical or mental health and age, and it may not "imply knowledge of medical information of a
user or user's family". A question counts: "Do you have diabetes?" is Meta's own rejected example, and so
is "Meet other seniors". The documented fix is to describe the product and its benefits ("New diabetes
treatment available"); "you" is allowed without an attribute. Meta says nothing about first-person framing
as a fix [PRIMARY · Meta Transparency Center, "Privacy Violations and Personal Attributes", and Business
Help Center 2557868957763449 · checked 2026-10-21].

**Meta — health and wellness.**
- Dietary, health and weight-loss products must target 18+.
- Banned: statements of inferiority about appearance, body parts or hygiene; a close-up pinching fat;
  results claimed from a wearable alone; timed-outcome promises without qualifiers; cure claims for the
  listed diseases (liver disease is not on that list, but §3 still applies).
- Allowed at 18+: people using a supplement, its impact, and the time it took.
[PRIMARY · Meta Transparency Center, "Health and Wellness", change log 22 Jul 2026 · checked 2026-10-21]

**TikTok (US) — weight management.** Weight-loss supplements "may be allowed" in the US and Canada at 18+,
with no claim that the product works without diet or exercise, no easy or guaranteed results and no body
shaming [PRIMARY · TikTok Ads Policy, "Weight Management and Body Image", last updated Aug 2026 · checked
2026-10-21]. The belief that TikTok bans them comes from its September 2020 announcement, which this
policy supersedes. Before-and-after product-effect comparisons are banned [PRIMARY · TikTok Ads Policy,
"Misleading and false content", Apr 2026 · checked 2026-10-21].

**AI disclosure on ads** is in §5's table: the TikTok toggle, and Meta's labelling.

**An older audience changes the disclosure test.** For an ad aimed at older consumers, "clear and
conspicuous" is judged from their perspective [PRIMARY · 16 CFR Part 255, §255.2 Example 10 · checked
2026-10-21]. Size and hold the disclosure for them, not for the editor.

## 6. The compliance gate — run this before export

Attach the completed checklist to the ✅ Branding Check subtask.

**Per script, before generation:**
- [ ] `scripts/claims_lint.py` run on the script; every hit either reframed or explicitly approved by the client with the approval recorded
- [ ] Every factual/program/offer detail (dates, eligibility, price, percentage) verified against the current brief — never carried forward from a previous episode
- [ ] No disease name attached to a non-drug product, in **words or imagery** — an anatomy shot of an abnormal organ included (§3)
- [ ] Health, weight and supplement ads: no question or assertion about the viewer's health or age, and the platform's own rules met (§5c)
- [ ] Net impression checked: read the script *with* the shot descriptions, not the words alone
- [ ] Where a result is shown, the typical result is stated at comparable prominence
- [ ] Required category disclaimer (e.g. the supplement disclaimer) present and legible
- [ ] The client's own approved-language sheet applied; conflicts with this file flagged, not resolved silently

**Per video, before export:**
- [ ] Synthetic presenter → dramatization disclosure burnt in from frame one, caption-sized, **top of frame inside the strict safe box** (`delivery-format.md` §3 — not the bottom third, which is platform UI)
- [ ] `claims_lint.py` run with **`--paid` or `--no-material-connection`** — one of them is true and a run with neither answers nothing (§2b.8 rule 7)
- [ ] Material connection disclosed where one exists — **on screen AND spoken**, from frame one, in plain words (§2b.3). Caption-only, toggle-only, hashtag-block and end-of-video placements all fail
- [ ] Anyone on camera who is an officer, manager, employee or agent of the brand says so, or the relationship is unmistakable in frame (§2b.5 — this one carries civil penalties)
- [ ] Every experience claim in the script matches what that speaker can actually attest (duration of use, results, "I switched from") (§2b.5)
- [ ] Disclosure re-verified **after** every reframe/repurpose, in the delivered aspect, against the platform's UI chrome — it is not inherited (§2b.6)
- [ ] Platform branded-content toggle set in addition to, never instead of, the in-video disclosure (§2b.7)
- [ ] No fabricated ratings, review counts, media placements, or endorsements on screen
- [ ] Platform AI-disclosure toggle set at upload (record which, in the export handoff note)
- [ ] Product and brand marks are the real assets, undistorted, correctly spelled
- [ ] Music and any archive/UGC footage licensed for this use, including paid usage if it will run as an ad

**Escalate rather than guess.** If a claim can't be verified against the brief or the approved-language
sheet, it goes back to the client as a question. A wrong wording in a claims-sensitive category is a
brand problem, not a creative note — and this is exactly what a client means when they say "small
wording mistakes can create problems."
