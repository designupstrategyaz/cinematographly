# Character and Props — the cast, the costume, the object in the hand

> **The emotional half of this file lives next door.** *What* goes in frame is here; *why an object
> moves the viewer's feeling* — externalise, instruct, withhold, date the beat, set the performance
> mode — is `emotional-journey.md` §5. Read that before choosing props for a beat that has to land,
> because **the valence of an emotional state is carried by the body and what it is doing with
> things, not by the face** (Aviezer 2012).

**When to read this:** you are designing a recurring character, writing the wardrobe or prop line of
a prompt, deciding what a character holds and where it sits in frame, designing a shot so a real
logo or product asset can be composited into it, or auditing generated frames for the drift that
makes episode 12 stop looking like episode 1.

Facts tiered per `research-quality.md`, checked **2026-08-28**. §1–§7 and §8.10 are durable craft.
**The rest of §8 is not:** reference limits, element taxonomies and per-model consistency behaviour
decay in about a quarter. On **2026-08-28** the file was merged with seven **local book
extractions** — Nusim, Glebas, Leandro, Rabiger, Jin Zhi, Wright, Dahlström — structured summaries
carrying verbatim quotation, **not the books**; the citation rule and the discount on each are in
§10.

`production-design.md` owns the room, the four-frame gate, the colour *system* and the wardrobe
token block — none of it restated here. This file goes deeper on **who the character is**, **what
the costume says**, and **props, especially placement**; set dressing appears only where it adds
something that file lacks.

## 1. Character design is staging, not decoration

`[SECONDARY · Thomas & Johnston, *The Illusion of Life*, pp. 47–69, via Wikipedia · checked
2026-08-28]` **Staging** is *"the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably
clear."* Everything below is an instrument of that sentence, not a rule of thumb. **Appeal**
*"corresponds to what would be called charisma in an actor"* and need not be sympathetic — villains
have it; `[SECONDARY · Knowles, Into Animation · checked 2026-08-28]` calls it emergent, *"at the
intersection of the other 11 Principles."* ⛔ **"Make it appealing" is a wasted prompt token:** it
decomposes into clear silhouette, simple face, asymmetry, a pose with a line of action, legible
intent. **Solid drawing** adds the anti-twins rule — mirrored sides read *"lifeless and dull."*

**The silhouette test is not a Disney law.** `[UNVERIFIED as a specific Disney-attributed rule]` —
no Disney or Preston Blair statement could be sourced; it descends from staging, which *is*
documented, so cite staging. It works because silhouette leaves only shape and pose: if those fail
to carry identity and intent, the read depends on information that vanishes at small size, in motion
blur, in backlight, or under compression — a **worst-case legibility audit**, and vertical
short-form is close to the worst case. Hence a gate, not an exercise.

**Design for the story function, not the picture.** `[PRIMARY · David Crank, production designer,
Pushing Pixels · fetched 2026-08-28]`: scenery should be *"not overpowering the story."* In a 15–30s
ad a character has exactly **one** function — sceptic, enthusiast, expert, obstacle. Encode it in
one silhouette-level feature; subtract the rest. Three competing ideas read as noise at six seconds.

**The governing test has a better formulation than "serves the story."** `[PRIMARY · Roberta Nusim
(pub.), *Art Direction: The Visual Language of Film*, AMPAS / Youth Media International teacher's
guide, 2003 — via a local extraction, read 2026-08-28]` quotes production designer Patrizia von
Brandenstein: *"the most beautiful ballroom on earth means nothing unless it helps the context of
the story."* The guide's own verification is the sharper half and the part worth stealing —
**removing or changing the element would change what the audience understands about the story.**
That is falsifiable where "serves the story" is a feeling, and it is the design-side twin of T20.
`[SECONDARY · Michael Rabiger, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics*, 4th ed. — via a local
extraction, read 2026-08-28]` names the failure it catches — **the "moment" scene**, beautiful,
atmospheric, advancing nothing — and prescribes cutting it. `[PRIMARY · Tim Leandro, *The Art of
Directing*, Routledge 2025 — via a local extraction, read 2026-08-28]` states it as an **"elemental
hierarchy"** with story above image, light, sound, design and music: if you are leaning on a
beautiful frame to carry emotion the story should have delivered, the fundamentals already failed.
*(Both books are directing books; their performance, blocking-card, coverage and selects material is
`direction.md`'s and is not duplicated here — only what bears on what is designed into the frame.)*

**Staging is attention management, and the failure has a name.** `[PRIMARY · Francis Glebas,
*Directing the Story*, Focal Press 2009 — via a local extraction, read 2026-08-28]`, a Disney
feature story artist, answers "what do directors direct?" with **attention**: it is grabbed by
motion, contrast, brightness, pointing and loudness (*"shine a light, jump up and down"*), and **the
conscious mind attends to one thing at a time**, so you present one thing and misdirect the rest.
His named failure is *"you didn't know where to look"* — two strong attractors at once. ⛔ **A
character design that out-contrasts the product is not a bold design; it is a staging error.** The
one-story-function rule above is this same instruction arriving from the design side.

**Two Glebas frames make §1 operational.** The **speaking metaphor** — tell it with pictures the way
you would speak it, one idea per image; and because most actions need three stages,
**anticipation, action, aftermath**, one story beat is a small series of frames, not one. And
***"every shot is a close-up"*** — whatever is in the frame is what you are saying *look at this*
about, so shot-size labels exist to talk to a crew while conceptually the frame always points at one
chosen idea. **Choose framing by asking what the shot is a close-up *of*.** His failure case is a
wide with no chosen subject — the princess too far away to read — which says nothing.

**On the silhouette caveat, a narrowing and no retraction.** Glebas teaches **clear silhouettes**
beside gesture drawing and the **line of action** in a pose, in a chapter on drawing for
storyboarding — so the practice is taught by a Disney feature story artist, not merely inferred from
staging. ⛔ **The §10 prohibition stands unchanged:** what was reached is the chapter's subject, not
a quotable statement, and nobody here states it as a *Disney rule*. What is genuinely new is the
**line of action** — it is the "what are they *doing*" half of T1, and a pose without one fails T1
even when the shape is unmistakable.

`[PRIMARY · Anna Dahlström, *Storytelling in Design*, O'Reilly 2020 — via a local extraction, read
2026-08-28]` supplies the reason none of this is optional. Writing about bots and voice UIs, she
observes that audiences **assign a personality to every actor whether or not the team designed
one.** The mechanism is this file's: **an unspecified element is not a neutral element, it is an
uncontrolled one** — which is why T20 subtraction is a stronger move than adding intent later.

**Tests. T1 fill** (black-fill on white: name the character, name what they are *doing* — either
failure means the shot is not staged) · **T2 cast-ID** (fills side by side at one height; two
confusable shapes is a design collision, not a rendering problem) · **T3 negative space** (readable
gaps between limbs and torso) · **T4 twins** (break bilateral symmetry) · **T31 one-idea** (read the
frame aloud as one sentence; if it needs an "and", it is carrying two) · **T32 close-up-of** (name
in one phrase what this shot is a close-up *of*; nothing nameable means nothing staged) · **T33
attractor count** (count the elements holding the strongest local contrast, brightness or motion —
more than one and the audience does not know where to look).

## 2. Shape and proportion — what the evidence supports

**What the industry teaches** `[SECONDARY · CGWire, "Character Shape Language (2026)" · checked
2026-08-28]`: circle = warm, square = stable, triangle = threatening — on an evidence base that is
**anecdotal and example-based**, citing characters, not studies. `[PRIMARY · Bancroft, *Creating
Characters with Personality*, 2006 · TOC verified 2026-08-28]` is the canonical text; its dedicated
chapters on read-age and cast groupings show the discipline treats **cast contrast** as a
first-class problem.

**What perception research licenses.** `[PRIMARY · Bar & Neta (2006) · abstract fetched 2026-08-28]`
hypothesised that *"sharp transitions in contour"* convey threat. `[PRIMARY · Munar et al. (2017) ·
fetched 2026-08-28]`: curved chosen **59% / 55% / 58%** in Spain, Mexico and Ghana at 80 ms forced
choice, no significant cross-cultural difference. `[PRIMARY · Cotter et al. (2017), "Curve Appeal" ·
fetched 2026-08-28]`: **moderated by art expertise and openness**, and **largely absent on familiar
shapes**, whose semantics override the contour cue.

> **The caveat, kept.** The bias is real and cross-cultural but **small** (5–9 points above chance),
> **weakens on familiar shapes**, and is **moderated by visual training**. That licenses *round
> biases mildly toward approachable, sharp mildly toward alert* — not the taxonomy. "Square =
> reliable," "triangle = villain" are **studio convention transmitted by example**: usable because
> audiences were trained on decades of animation applying them consistently, but conventional, not
> perceptual, and weaker outside a Western-animation audience. **Use shape as within-series
> contrast** — this one is rounder than that one — never as absolute semantics.

**Proportion is the strongest evidence in the canon.** `[PRIMARY · Gould, "A Biological Homage to
Mickey Mouse," 1980 · PDF fetched 2026-08-28]` measured Mickey with calipers across fifty years: eye
size **27% → 42%** of head length, head **42.7% → 48.1%** of body, cranial vault **71.7% → 95.6%**.
The mechanism is Lorenz's *Kindchenschema* and Gould's claim that *"babyish features tend to elicit
strong feelings of affection"* — named mechanism, measured drift, in the predicted direction.
`[UNVERIFIED · studio convention, no source found]` the heads-tall ladder: ~2–3 mascot, 4–5
child/cute-adult, 6 stylised adult, 7.5–8 realistic, 8.5+ heroic.

**Tests. T5 primary form** (two leads reducing to the same primitive at the same size are
interchangeable in six seconds) · **T6 contour count** (a "warm" character with more sharp
direction-changes than the "threatening" one is fighting itself) · **T7 heads-tall** (measured *in
the delivered frame*, same number every shot — the commonest failure that reads as "different
character" even when the face matches) · **T8 eye-to-head** (locked as a number; up for sympathy,
down for authority) · **T9 cranial vault** (growing skull mass above the eyeline is the generator
drifting the age).

## 3. The 9:16 constraint — which silhouette you are actually designing

`[PRIMARY · Szita & Rooney (2021), *i-Perception* 12(1) · fetched 2026-08-28]` compared a 5.5" phone
at 30–60 cm with a 47.3" projection at 180 cm: visual angle **11.52–22.8°** vs **32.4°**, and **gaze
dispersion significantly lower on smartphones**, fixations concentrated centrally. **On a phone the
audience does not scan the frame; they look at the middle** — off-centre detail is largely
*unfixated*. `[SECONDARY · CheckSafe.Zone TikTok safe-area guide · fetched 2026-08-28 · third-party,
NOT TikTok's own documentation]` for 1080×1920: top **200**, bottom **334** organic / **450**
in-feed ad, right **140**, left **86** px — about **850×1386** safe.

> **The composite rule, and it is a design decision, not a framing one.** The reliable canvas is
> that central 850×1386, and within it the central column carries the fixations — a frame in which a
> full-figure wide shot is nearly useless. **Character design must survive at head-and-shoulders to
> waist-up, so the silhouette that matters is head, hair, shoulder line and collar, not the
> full-body silhouette a character sheet emphasises.** Generate the turnaround anyway; audit the
> head.

**Two independent arguments land on the same frame.** Szita & Rooney's fixation data says the phone
viewer looks at the middle and does not scan; `[PRIMARY · Glebas]`'s *"every shot is a close-up"*
says every frame already points at one idea whether or not you chose it. **In 9:16 those collapse
into a single instruction: the frame has room for one idea, and it is the one in the central
column.** The head-and-shoulders rule is what that does to character design; the same instruction
applied to the product is the whole reason an insert exists.

**Tests. T10 thumbnail** (at 120 px wide: character, emotion and product each still identifiable) ·
**T11 overlay** (safe-zone mask on every key frame, nothing load-bearing under UI) · **T12 head
silhouette** (if identity lives below the chest it will not survive this format) · **T13 squint**
(blurred heavily, the figure still separates as a value mass).

A cover image built from one face chest-up and two words (`discovery-and-search.md` §2) is held to T10
too: the face still reads at 120 px wide.

## 4. Costume as characterisation

`[PRIMARY · Deborah Nadoolman Landis, costume designer, Daily Bruin Q&A · fetched 2026-08-28]`
*"Costume designers don't need to know how to sew, we need to know how to read"* — the job is script
analysis, and the costume is a **"second skin." Fashion is designed to be *looked at*; costume is
designed to be *looked through*** — the whole answer to "reads instantly vs reads as a costume."

**What a garment encodes** `[PRIMARY · Ruth E. Carter (*Black Panther*), RogerEbert.com · fetched
2026-08-28]`: **rank via craft quality, not insignia** — armour *"crafted like jewellery... and also
a rank"*; **group identity by colour block**, a colour script applied to bodies; **individual
identity inside a uniform** — *"a special three pieces"* per character over a shared look, the most
directly transferable rule here; and **texture as meaning**.

**Change over time moves one variable inside a held system.** `[SECONDARY · Kathleen Detoro on
*Breaking Bad*, via tvdressing · fetched 2026-08-28]` Walt runs khaki → green *"symbolizing his
growing life"* → black; Jesse is fixed at *"black, white, yellow and red"*; Saul clashes for *"the
guy who has a lot of money but who has no taste."* Walt's *silhouette* barely changes — the
**value** moves. Hold the vocabulary, move the colour: that survives compression, phone screens and
generative drift where a redesign does not.

`[UNVERIFIED · synthesis from Carter and Detoro, not a stated rule of either]` **value** carries
status and moral position and is the most robust channel; **hue** carries faction; **saturation**
carries energy and self-regard; **texture and fit** carry competence and class — and texture is the
first thing lost at phone scale and the last thing a generator gets right, so **exaggerate it at
design time**.

**A costume reads as a costume when the audience identifies the *design decision* before the
*person*.** `[UNVERIFIED · my formulation, consistent with Landis and Crank]` Causes, in
generated-frame frequency order: too-new fabric; full-set coherence, every item from one "look",
which real people never achieve; a doubled signifier (overalls *and* wrench *and* oil smear *and*
name patch); and nothing wrong anywhere.

**Continuity in live action is a photographic register, not memory.** `[PRIMARY · IATSE Local 489
Wardrobe Department PDF · fetched 2026-08-28]` the emphasis is theirs: *"All costumers take
continuity photos of the actors and BG. **THIS IS ONE OF THE MOST IMPORTANT THINGS WE DO.**"*
`[PRIMARY · Blauw Films on the costume department · fetched 2026-08-28]` the **Costume Bible** holds
pictures *"taken on every shooting day, before and after every scene."* ⛔ **Never treat a saved
wardrobe token as continuity** — the token is the specification, the register is the enforcement,
and in generation the register does not exist until you build it (§8).

**Texture is a design channel in its own right — and the canonical example assigns it to faction,
not to class.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim guide]` on Catherine Martin's *Romeo + Juliet*: Capulets and
Montagues are separated by **dark reds and blacks in leather and denim against bright Hawaiian
patterns** — material and surface doing faction work alongside colour. ⛔ **That contradicts the
channel assignment above**, which reserves hue for faction and texture for competence and class.
Kept, not reconciled. The honest reading: **texture carries faction when the two groups differ in
material culture** — street against court, trade against office, field against boardroom — and when
it does, it does hue's job on a channel that survives a shared palette. The file's assignment stays
the default because it degrades gracefully; Martin's is what a strong material contrast licenses.

**Saturation has a stated register, from a second tradition.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim guide]`:
**saturated, deep colours convey seriousness and intensity; bright colours suggest lightness and
delicacy** — an independent statement of the "saturation carries energy and self-regard" line above.

**Design the costume in greyscale first.** The guide's black-and-white problem is a designer in
monochrome having to *"anticipate how set colours translate into tones of black, gray and white"* —
the grayscale rendering of colour is **a separate design problem**, not a by-product. That is the
mechanism behind value being the most robust channel, and it converts into a method: **decide the
cast's values before their hues.** Two costumes that separate in hue but not in value are one
costume at 120 px, under compression, in backlight, and in any grade that pulls saturation. The
compositing side reaches the same procedure from the other end — `[PRIMARY · Steve Wright,
*Compositing Visual Effects*, Focal Press — via a local extraction, read 2026-08-28]` sets black and
white points **on a monochrome version of the frame**, precisely so colour cannot distract from
value.

**Tests. T14 one-signifier** (more than one element encoding the same fact is a costume, not
clothing) · **T15 wear** (collar, cuffs, knees, shoe creases — zero wear reads as a mannequin) ·
**T16 three-personals** (three specific items that are *theirs*, named in the bible, restated every
prompt) · **T17 value ladder** (order the cast light→dark; a mismatch with the status order means
the costume argues against the script) · **T34 greyscale** (desaturate the whole frame to zero —
every character must still separate from every other character and from the background; hue-only
separation does not survive delivery).

## 5. Set dressing — only what `production-design.md` does not say

**A space is designed by history, not decoration.** `[PRIMARY · Alex DiGerlando, production
designer, Pushing Pixels · fetched 2026-08-28]` a designer *"designs a space for everything that's
happened there before the point in time when the story is being told."* You accumulate **the residue
of a person's past**, not their taste.

**Fidelity is allocated by shot type.** `[UNVERIFIED · standard art-department usage; no first-party
definition obtained]` **hero dressing** is touched or seen close and must withstand scrutiny;
**mid-ground** need only be right in silhouette, colour and material class; **background** only in
value and density. DiGerlando's *"spread the resources over the whole show"* becomes a **credit
rule**: an adjective spent on a background object is one not spent on the object in the hand. And a
set is **a machine for generating angles** — `[PRIMARY · Richard Hoover, production designer,
Pushing Pixels · fetched 2026-08-28]` the deliverable is to *"set out this agenda"* and share it
with the director and DP. The metric is not "would someone live here" but how many distinct legible
shots come out without redressing: 5–8, all readable at 850×1386.

**Figure/ground is a contrast decision in one of three channels — pick which.** `[SECONDARY · Bruce
Block, *The Visual Story*, via a practitioner summary · fetched 2026-08-28]` *"contrast... increases
visual intensity and affinity decreases visual intensity."* **Tone** is most robust and survives
compression and grading; **colour** second; **focus/depth** least, since AI depth-of-field is
inconsistent shot to shot. ⛔ **Say the value relationship in the prompt — generators default to
mid-value everything, and this is fixed in design, never in the grade.**

**Architecture is decided before decoration and has three named variables.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim
guide]`: the designer first fixes **volume** — the overall size and shape of the space, a large
rectangular hall (Xanadu) or a claustrophobic enclosure (the *U-571* submarine) — then **scale**,
relative size used to evoke feeling about a space and the people in it, the worked example being the
outsized *Gone with the Wind* staircase reading as Rhett's excess and his power over Scarlett.
**Scale is a character tool wearing an architecture costume:** it states status without touching the
costume or the script, and it works entirely through the ratio between the figure and the built
element behind it. ⛔ In a prompt that ratio is a number, not an adjective — "a doorway twice her
height" survives where "grand" does not. The third variable is **perspective**: *"just like
painters, production designers exploit perspective"* to direct the eye to a chosen spot and to build
depth. In
9:16 the converging lines available are few and mostly vertical, so **the eye-direction work a wide
frame does with perspective has to be done in this section's other two channels — value and
layering.**

**The enemies of design are named, and two of them are silhouette failures.** `[PRIMARY · Glebas]`
gives the **Design Equation** — *elements* (points, lines, planes, edges, shapes, values, sizes,
colours) organised by *principles* (balance, position, dominance, unity, repetition, contrast,
symmetry, rhythm) produce *effects* (light, depth, volume, motion, temperature, atmosphere) —
singles out **contrast** as the principle that matters most, and names the two failure states:
**boredom** (evenness, predictability, **symmetry**) and **confusion** (bad **tangents**, crowding,
no centre of attention, spottiness). Symmetry-as-boredom is a second source for T4. **Tangents are
the addition**: an edge that merely *touches* another edge — a hairline grazing a doorframe, a
shoulder meeting a horizon line, an outline kissing the frame edge — fuses two shapes into one and
destroys exactly the separation T1 depends on. Generators produce them constantly, because nothing
in the model is tracking silhouette.

**Depth killers are a checklist, not a feeling.** `[PRIMARY · Glebas]`, crediting Bruce Block for
the contrast approach: depth is built from overlapping shapes, diminishing sizes, converging lines,
value and a consistent horizon, and four choices destroy it — **lines parallel to the frame edge**,
**wrong relative sizes** (broken size constancy), **all-black shadows or blown-white highlights**
(which read as *holes* and as *pasted-on*), and **objects that do not share a common horizon**. Two
notes. First, this is an independent route into `photorealism-physics.md`'s clipping material: what
a compositor calls a crushed black, a story artist calls a hole in the picture, and both mean the
element has stopped belonging. Second, **flatness is legitimate when chosen** — Glebas treats depth
killers as deliberate contrast material — but in generation it is almost never chosen, so treat all
four as defects until you can say why one is there.

**The set is a machine for angles because the camera moves — and that is where craft and stack
diverge.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim guide]` states the distinction plainly: what separates production
designers from their theatre equivalents *"is the camera"*, since every set must accommodate its
changing three-dimensional view at various angles, distances and lighting — the historical leap from
Méliès's flat painted backdrops to the integrated three-dimensional sets of *Intolerance*. `[PRIMARY
· Leandro]` puts a figure on the requirement: a location must offer **photographable interest in
every direction, ideally 360°, filling the frame** — and it can only be found by physically walking
it.

> **Named, not smoothed: that requirement and this stack are incompatible.** §8.4's finding is that
> a generator has no three-dimensional object to walk around. Nusim's answer to the same problem in
> 2003 was the **scale model** — build the space so it can be visualised before anything is
> committed. **You have no model and cannot build one**, so the requirement survives only as a
> *planning* constraint: choose environments whose interest is concentrated where you will actually
> point the camera, and accept that a 360°-interesting set is not something you can verify, only
> something you re-invent per angle. This is the strongest argument in the file for **fewer
> environments, harder reuse.**

**Leandro adds two facts that invert the usual instinct.** First, **all film space is invention** —
one coherent "virtual" location is routinely assembled from several separate real ones, which means
**spatial continuity is a claim the edit makes, not a property the location has.** That is directly
useful here, where a "location" is a set of images which need only agree with each other *in the
cuts you actually make*. Second, **the set exists to serve the illusion, not to be real**: his
example is windows left unglazed to kill reflections and read as depth, where making them real
destroys the shot. ⛔ **Do not generalise this into a licence for the model.** A designer's
falsification is aimed *at* the camera and requires knowing exactly what the camera does; a
generator falsifies by default and in random directions. The licence belongs to the designer; the
model gets `photorealism-physics.md`.

**Set decoration characterises collectively, and the diagnostic is smaller than a room.** `[PRIMARY
· Nusim guide]`: audiences infer character from environment, so *"how a character decorates a room
or what is in their medicine chest"* can reveal as much as dialogue — the worked example is a
lawyer's office where takeout cartons, a toothbrush and a blank desk calendar say *failing, sleeping
here*. The verification is the usable part: **a viewer reading only the décor can describe the
character's situation accurately.** Three objects chosen for what they imply beat a fully dressed
room — which is also the only version of set dressing that survives §8.4's "under-specify the
background".

**Research to credibility, then deviate on purpose.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim guide]`: designers research
period or future until the world is credible, but *"sometimes, however, designers choose drama over
realism"* — selecting details that are not literally accurate but are emotionally true. **This
contradicts the file's default posture** and `photorealism-physics.md`'s, and the contradiction is
real, not verbal: credibility is the floor and expressive deviation is a deliberate override
available only to someone who knows what the accurate version was. In generation the practical rule
inverts, because **the model deviates from accuracy constantly and never deliberately**: spend the
deviation budget once, name it in the bible, negate the rest.

**Tests. T18 value-behind-head** (background value behind the head vs hair/shoulder value; small ΔL
and the figure is dissolving) · **T19 history** (name what happened to three objects before this
scene, or the room is decorated, not designed) · **T20 subtraction** (remove one item, repeat until
removing anything hurts) · **T21 angle count** (under five legible shots means re-prompting the
environment, where drift enters — a planning test, not a QA one) · **T22 depth layers** (three
identifiable layers, or the frame reads flat at phone scale) · **T35 depth killers** (check all
four: frame-parallel lines, broken size constancy, clipped black or blown white, no shared
horizon) · **T36 tangents** (trace every silhouette edge for a point where it merely touches
another edge — a fuse means re-stage, not re-grade) · **T37 volume and scale** (state the space's
volume and the figure-to-architecture ratio as numbers; if neither was decided, it is a backdrop,
not a set).

## 6. Props — and specifically placement

**The categories exist to allocate effort.** `[PRIMARY · "hero" definition, HowToFilmSchool ·
fetched 2026-08-28]` a hero is an element *"intended to be seen clearly on camera and withstand
close scrutiny"* — meaningful only because **non-hero versions exist**. `[SECONDARY · Saturation.io
prop taxonomy · fetched 2026-08-28]`: handheld, personal (*"help define a character's
personality"*), set, practical, hero, background — with the boundary that a prop is *"portable and
handled by actors"* where set pieces *"are not designed to move."* `[UNVERIFIED · "action prop" /
"stunt prop" / "breakaway" as formal categories]` widely used for the lightweight or safe duplicate,
absent from both first-party sources reached: use the words, do not attribute them.

> **Why it matters.** The category assigns **design effort, number of duplicates, and whose
> responsibility.** Handled ⇒ props, multiples, continuity tracked; not handled ⇒ set decoration,
> untracked. In generation, miscategorising is how you spend the fidelity budget on a background
> vase while the object in the character's hand changes shape between clips.

**The breakdown is a per-object timeline.** `[SECONDARY · Wikipedia, "Property master" · fetched
2026-08-28]` the property master is responsible for *"properly placing"* props and builds a
breakdown of *"the logical progression of each prop throughout the story."* Steal that phrase: a
per-object timeline parallel to the wardrobe breakdown, tracking state — full/empty, clean/dirty,
new/broken, present/absent — beat by beat. It is what lets a prop become a story beat, and it exists
in no generation tool.

**Designing a hero prop for the close-up.** `[PRIMARY-adjacent · derived from the hero definition,
Hoover on HD scrutiny, Carter on armour-as-jewellery]` The close-up is the only shot where a static
object fills the frame. It must be **legible at both scales** — reading at waist-up so the audience
knows what it is *before* the insert, and still rewarding the insert; solve that with **one bold
form plus one fine texture**. It needs a **handled surface** (wear at the grip, patina at contact
points — on HD, absence of wear is a lie), **a single focal detail** (one mark, one chip, one
label), and a **brand legibility budget** — the hero prop is usually the product and its close-up is
the shot the spot exists to deliver, so composite it from the real asset.

**Placement is how a prop writes the blocking.** `[SECONDARY · Wikipedia, "Blocking (stage)"]`
defines blocking as *"the precise arrangement of actors to facilitate the performance"* — but **it
contains no definition of "business" and does not connect props to it**, flagged rather than papered
over. `[UNVERIFIED · standard directing practice, no named source found]` for the following:

- **A prop motivates a move.** Actors do not cross a room because the director wants a new angle;
  they cross because the kettle boiled. Put the object at the destination and the blocking writes
  itself.
- **A prop fixes an eyeline** — and head angle decides whether the face reads at all.
- **A prop creates a reason to turn.** Turning is how one setup yields a second angle; an object
  behind and to one side buys a profile-to-three-quarter turn. This pays double in generation, where
  a new *angle* is a new invention (§8.4) but a *turn* is interpolation.
- **A prop sets the axis of a two-shot** — a table, a cup, the product between two characters
  defines the line.
- **Placement is the entire difference between a prop and dressing.** A cup on the far counter is
  dressing; the same cup within arm's reach is a prop, because it is available to the performance.

**Something to do with the hands.** `[UNVERIFIED · attribution]` universally taught, no named source
found; the nearest attributable adjacent statement is `[SECONDARY · Michael Caine, *Acting in Film*,
via No Film School · fetched 2026-08-28]` — *"don't mess up the continuity"* on smoking — which is
the *cost*, not the benefit. Take the mechanism instead: an actor with nothing to hold has only face
and posture, so tension surfaces as self-touching or stillness that reads as *acting*, while an
object absorbs it and converts it into character information. **In generation the rule pays again
for an unrelated reason:** hands are the most failure-prone region of a generated figure, and an
occupied hand is markedly more stable than an empty one.

**A prop becomes a story beat when its state changes and the change is the information** — full
glass → empty, wrapped → opened, off → on. **In a 20-second ad there is room for exactly one, and it
should be the product's.**

**The single crucial prop is a documented category, not a flourish.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim guide]`:
props usually work collectively, but *sometimes an individual prop is crucial to the story* — the
witch's hourglass in *The Wizard of Oz*, the sled in *Citizen Kane* — and the verification adds a
clause the file lacked: **the object is immediately recognisable and its meaning is legible when it
recurs.** A hero prop that appears once is a prop; **one that recurs and is recognised is a story
object**, and across a series the recurring object does brand work for free.

**A prop's state change must answer a question the audience is already holding.** `[PRIMARY ·
Glebas]`: *"the secret of storytelling is story-delaying"* — raise a question the audience cares
about and withhold the answer, because the gap is what holds attention. His failure case is
answering early, which drains a scene *"like a majestic ship when the wind dies."* So **T27's one
state change is an answer, and it has to arrive after its question** — which is usually posed by the
object's first appearance. `[PRIMARY · Dahlström]` states the same as the working question of
dramaturgy — ***"what do you want your audience to know and when?"*** — with the sharper evidence
that re-ordering a reveal changes what an audience *feels* about a character, not merely what they
know. **Reveal order is a design decision about the object, not only a script decision.**

**Dramatic irony is available to a prop and costs one insert.** `[PRIMARY · Glebas]`: uneven
knowledge is the engine — an audience knowing more than the character produces sustained suspense,
where surprise is a one-time jolt. **Show the object to the audience before the character finds
it.** In a 15–30 second spot this is one of the very few structural devices that actually fits, it
needs no dialogue, and it is a single frame.

**Design the hero prop so it can be composited at all.** Chrome, glass, transparency, fringe and
motion-blurred edges are precisely the cases that make an element impossible to isolate cleanly —
see **§8.10**, which is the art-direction half of a discipline whose physics half belongs to
`photorealism-physics.md`.

**Tests. T23 category audit** (label every object hero / handled / dressing — more than one hero
means no hero) · **T24 reach** (inside the arm radius is a prop and is continuity-tracked, outside
is dressing; is the hero prop inside it?) · **T25 hands** (empty hands get something to hold or get
cropped out) · **T26 eyeline source** (the eyeline must terminate on an object in frame or a defined
off-screen position — unmotivated eyelines are the fastest way a generated shot reads as fake) ·
**T27 state** (the hero prop's state changes exactly once) · **T38 question-then-answer** (name the
question the prop's first appearance poses and the frame where its state change answers it; an
answer that lands before its question is a dead beat) · **T39 matte-hardness** (chrome, glass,
transparency or fringe *on the silhouette edge* means the object can never be cleanly isolated —
move the reflective material inside the outline).

## 7. Colour held across a series

The palette *system* is `production-design.md` §3 and the tokens are `brand-system.md`; this is only
about **holding it across beats, characters and episodes.**

**The colour script** `[SECONDARY · Amid Amidi, *The Art of Pixar*, Animated Views · fetched
2026-08-28]` maps *"the color, lighting, emotion and moods in a film"*; Ralph Eggleston made the
first at Pixar for *Toy Story* in pastel, chosen as *"a very fast and efficient medium"*, and Amidi
is honest about the ceiling — *"By itself, a color script won't make or break a film."* It is
**low-fidelity by design** and made **during story development, not after**. For a 20-second ad:
**6–10 postage-stamp swatches, one per beat, fifteen minutes** — the only artefact that shows the
arc's shape **before credits are spent**. Do it at the storyboard gate (`pm-workflow.md`).

**Assign a palette, hold it, move exactly one.** Detoro's system is legible only because Jesse, Saul
and Skyler are fixed while Walt moves; Carter's tribes are legible because each block is held. **If
everything drifts, nothing signifies.** A fixed 3-colour palette with hex per recurring character
and location; movement reserved for whichever element carries the arc.

**Restriction is a power technique, not a modesty one.** `[SECONDARY · Bruce Block]` a restricted
palette is *affinity* of colour, which by his law **decreases intensity and therefore creates
headroom** — one out-of-band colour becomes maximally intense at zero cost, which is where the brand
accent goes. `[UNVERIFIED · argument, not a sourced claim]` this matters more in brand work than in
film: brand colours are a fixed input; **recognition across a series is the product**, and at
thumbnail size in a feed a held palette beats face and beats logo; and a tight palette **narrows the
generator's search space**, reducing shot-to-shot variance.

**Colour symbolism is culture-specific, and the palette advice above quietly assumes an audience.**
`[PRIMARY · Nusim guide]`: in Western cultures **red denotes danger and white purity**, while **in
Chinese culture white is the colour of death and red signifies happiness and health**; the guide's
verification is the operative line — *the colour's symbolic reading in the target audience matches
the intended meaning.* ⛔ **This does not weaken the held-palette argument; it splits it in two.** A
palette's **recognition** value across a series is audience-independent: the same three colours read
as *the same brand* to anyone. A palette's **meaning** is not. Hold the palette for recognition, and
check any *semantic* claim — this colour means danger, purity, celebration — against the audience
the spot is actually served to, which in a feed is an audience you did not choose. It is the caution
§2 applies to shape, arriving from a second direction.

**Palette contrast between sections is where the narrative work happens.** `[PRIMARY · Nusim
guide]`: *A Little Princess* sets the bright oranges and purples of Sara's past against the greys
and browns of her present; Richard Sylbert chose white buildings throughout *Chinatown* because
*"white makes you feel hotter,"* supporting the drought. Two things the file did not have. First,
**colour can imply a physical state, not only an emotional one** — heat, cold, damp, dust — which is
the cheapest available way to make a generated environment read as a place rather than a render.
Second, the mechanism is **contrast between palettes inside one piece**, so a colour script whose
beats do not contrast is a swatch list, not a script. ⛔ A uniform palette across emotionally
distinct sections wastes the channel — and in a 20-second spot the sections are the problem beat and
the resolution beat.

> **A tension worth holding rather than resolving.** This section argues restriction as a power
> technique: affinity of colour lowers intensity and buys headroom for the accent. `[PRIMARY ·
> Glebas]` names **evenness and predictability as boredom**, the first enemy of design. Both are
> right and they bound each other: **restriction earns its headroom only if the headroom is spent.**
> A held palette with no accent event — or with the accent everywhere — has bought quiet and sold
> nothing. The audit is T30 plus one question: *where in these six beats does the restriction pay
> off?*

**Tests. T28 swatch** (five dominant colours per frame, all in the series palette, accent exactly
where intended) · **T29 series strip** (every shot's dominant swatch side by side reads as one
system with an arc, not a random walk) · **T30 reserved accent** (the accent appears nowhere it
should not).

## 8. The AI translation layer

**8.1 The framing.** Everything above is a **specification discipline**, executed by people and
enforced by a photographic register. In generation the **specification transfers completely and the
enforcement transfers not at all.** You rebuild the register by hand, and nothing in the stack will
remind you it is missing.

**8.2 Character consistency — what is documented.** Reference images dominate. `[PRIMARY · Runway,
"Introducing Gen-4" · fetched 2026-08-28]` claims Gen-4 *"generates consistent characters, objects
and locations across scenes from a single reference image"*, with no fine-tuning. The operational
detail from `[PRIMARY · Runway Help Centre, Gen-4 Image References · fetched 2026-08-28]` is worth
more than the claim: **up to three References per generation**, session-temporary unless you *"tag
to save"*; a good character reference has *"natural, even lighting"* and a *"neutral subject
expression"* — a *"'blank canvas' that simplifies transformation"*; **cover existing faces with
black boxes before uploading**; for environments, prompt the same location for *"various angles,
focal points, and objects"*; and **chain results as new references**. `[PRIMARY · Scenario KB on
Gen-4 References · fetched 2026-08-28]` adds `image_1`/`image_2`/`image_3` labels so the model knows
*"which inputs should influence the output"*, a **720×720 / 1280×720** input cap, and annotation
with arrows to direct gaze. `[PRIMARY · Google Developers Blog on Veo 3.1 · fetched 2026-08-28]` —
*"up to 3 reference images of a character, object, or scene"*, plus first-and-last-frame
interpolation and scene extension from the previous clip's final second.

`[PRIMARY · Higgsfield MCP tool documentation, in-environment, checked 2026-08-28]` on trained
identity models:

| | **Elements** (`show_reference_elements`) | **Soul** (`show_characters`, action=train) |
|---|---|---|
| Input / time | 1 image, instant | 5–20 photos of one person, ~10 min training |
| Subjects per generation | **multiple** `<<<element_id>>>` placeholders | **one** soul |
| Categories | **character / environment / prop** | person only |

> **The most important architectural fact here:** the platform's own taxonomy for persistent
> reusable references is **character / environment / prop** — precisely the production-design triad.
> **That is a digital art department, and the closest thing to a continuity register the stack
> offers.** Name tokens after bible entries, not after the prompt that made them. ⛔ **A trained
> identity model handles one subject per generation** — any two-character shot must use the
> multi-reference path, so design the blocking around that, not against it.

> **A reference image alone is under-specified, and a compositing supervisor said so first.**
> `[PRIMARY · Wright]`: when many shots of one element are split across artists, handing out a
> **hero still** fails, because there are *"a thousand ways to achieve that one look."* His fix is a
> **hero frame plus the script that produced it** — one shared recipe everyone starts from, varied
> only to fit each shot's light. `[PRIMARY · Jin Zhi, *Invisible Visual Effects*, Routledge — via a
> local extraction, read 2026-08-28]` makes it a pipeline rule: departments ship **packages
> containing the generating scripts, not just the renders**, so a downstream artist can reproduce
> the result without a round trip. **Translated: a saved Element or tagged Reference is half the
> artefact. Save the frame *and* the prompt, seed, model, version and settings that produced it**,
> in the bible, beside the image. §8.7's "reference the nouns, prompt the verbs" is right and
> incomplete — the noun is an image *plus its recipe*, or the next person (you, in a month)
> re-derives it differently and calls the difference drift.

**8.3 The character sheet, and the babyface correction.** `[PRIMARY · Higgsfield bundled
`character-sheet` workflow, SKILL.md v1.0, read in-environment 2026-08-28]` states four principles
that restate §1–§2 in AI-native form: *"Photorealism means anti-retouch, never idealized"* (pores,
natural asymmetry, matte skin); original characters only; *"Mature facial structure for adults"*;
and ***"Consistency carries forward"*** — *"Change only what's requested; restate the rest so
nothing silently drifts."* Slot order matters because earlier tokens weigh more: composition →
*"identical original [subject] on all views"* → seamless ground → identity → face → eyes → hair →
realism module → body → **wardrobe head-to-toe, top through bag, no gaps** → lighting → negative
tail. Presets map onto the traditional model sheet: split-screen, **turnaround**, expression sheet,
outfit variations.

> **Gould's finding shows up here as a production defect.** The workflow names mature facial
> structure as *"a recurring correction point"* — generators drift toward juvenile proportions
> unless prevented. Neoteny is not a style you pick; it is a bias you either use deliberately or
> negate in the negative tail.

**8.3b ⚡ The house sheet, and the reject test that is about LIGHT, not likeness.**
`[SECONDARY · practitioner corpus]` fixes a single sheet format for every character and every prop
in a film, and it is worth adopting as is because it makes sheets comparable across a cast:

> **A plain grey background; three panels side by side with thin dark dividers: a full-body FRONT,
> a full-body BACK, and a separate CLOSE-UP HEADSHOT.**

Front and back give the model the wardrobe head-to-toe with nothing inferred; the separate headshot
gives the face at a resolution a full-body panel never has. That third panel is what makes the sheet
work as an identity lock at MCU, and it is the one most often left out.

⚡ **But the two body panels are HEADLESS.** `[SECONDARY · course notes]`

⚠️ **SUPERSEDES IN PLACE** (2026-09-17) — previously: "One identical person across all three." The
model cannot hold a face identical across three panels at three scales, so the instruction produced
sheets carrying two disagreeing faces — which is worse than one, because every downstream generation
then gets to choose. — The course specifies each body panel as headless: nothing visible above the
collar, ghost-mannequin style. The sheet carries **exactly one rendering of the face**,
in the headshot panel. The body panels carry wardrobe and nothing else.

This corrects the obvious-seeming instruction *"one identical person across all three"*, which this
file carried until the course contradicted it. The instruction is not wrong as a wish — it is
wrong as a **request**, because the model cannot hold a face identical across three panels at three
scales, and a sheet where the headshot face and the full-body face disagree is **worse than a sheet
with one face**: it hands every downstream generation two candidate identities and lets it pick.
The failure is documented in the corpus — a character whose close-up and full-body faces did not
match, and whose shots then drifted between them.

> ⚡ **The rule generalises past sheets: when a panel will only supply conflicting evidence, remove
> the evidence.** Keep the best face as the only identity source, and let the headless outfit
> panels carry the wardrobe. A reference is not a mood board — every pixel in it is an instruction, so a
> second, worse rendering of an attribute you have already locked is a **cost**, not a bonus.
> Applies to props too: a prop sheet showing the same object at two finishes is a sheet that will
> return both.

**The corollary is the SUB-REFERENCE.** When one detail inside an approved asset keeps drifting,
do not reopen the asset — crop or re-derive **that detail alone** as its own named reference and
bind it in addition. The corpus does this for a small detail inside an approved object whose marking was
locked but whose shape kept morphing: the fix was a dedicated detail element (`@object_detail`),
defined as that detail and its marking and scoped to that detail only, extracted from footage
already approved, not generated fresh. Re-deriving from approved footage keeps the sub-reference in the same world as its
parent; generating it fresh starts a second negotiation about the same object. See §8.3c for the
scope suffix every such definition carries.

⛔ **The sheet stage is a SELECTION stage, not a specification stage.** A sheet costs well under a
credit; the model returns a different person every run. **Generate a batch and pick** — the move is
not to nail the character on the first try. This is the one place in this skill where divergence is
cheaper than precision, and it does not violate the quality floor (SKILL.md §4.11): every candidate
is generated at full tier, and you are choosing between takes, not between drafts.

**And most of a batch fails on LIGHTING, not on likeness.** This is the finding worth carrying:

| Reject | What you see |
|---|---|
| ⛔ **Shadow side** | one side of the face falls into shadow — unusable however good the face is |
| ⛔ **Hair glare** | a harsh specular catches the hair |
| ⛔ **Underexposure** | the sheet renders dark, detail lost in the shadow |
| ✅ **Keep** | soft, even, near-shadowless wrap-around light · **no glare on the hair** · a visible **catchlight in the eyes** · the headshot panel reading as the same person as the source, and the two headless panels agreeing on the wardrobe |

> **Why this is a gate and not a preference.** Every downstream shot inherits the sheet's lighting
> as part of the identity it locks. A dark or glary sheet does not merely look worse — **it poisons
> every generation made from it**, and the cost lands forty shots later as an identity that will not
> hold under a different key. The sheet is the cheapest thing in the pipeline to redo and the most
> expensive thing to have got wrong. ⚡ Screen the batch on light FIRST, likeness second.

Once a sheet reads clean: lock it, save it as an Element, and **name it** — from then on the name is
what the prompt carries (`@hero`), not a description. See `prompting-craft.md` §11d for the
`not:` scope every sheet-derived reference needs, because the plain grey and the panel layout travel
with the identity unless the prompt says they do not.

**8.3c ⚡ The scope suffix — `not:` moved to the point of DEFINITION.**
`[SECONDARY · course notes]` writes every element in a standing `— REFERENCE DEFINITIONS —` block at
the head of the prompt, and **every entry ends with what it is allowed to supply**. In our own
template form:

| Element kind | Definition ends with |
|---|---|
| a character (`@hero`) | `— character appearance only. Reference.` |
| an object (`@object_sheet`) | `— object appearance only. Reference.` |
| a detail sub-reference (`@object_detail`) | `— <detail> reference, that detail only. Reference.` |
| a location (`@location_1`) | `— location and mood reference. Reference.` |

This is the same control as the per-shot `not:` (`prompting-craft.md` §11d) moved one level up, and
the move is the point. A `not:` written at the point of **use** has to be retyped in every shot that
binds the element, and the shot where someone forgets is the shot where the plate's grey seamless or
the sheet's neutral pose walks into the frame. A scope suffix written at the point of **definition**
travels with the element to every prompt that carries the block. ⚡ **Per-shot scoping fails at the
one shot that forgets; per-element scoping cannot.**

Two properties make the suffix work as written:

- **It is POSITIVE.** `character appearance only` names the one channel that transfers, rather
  than enumerating channels that must not — and an enumeration is always incomplete. Use `not:` for
  a specific leak you have actually seen; use the suffix for the default.
- **It names a CHANNEL, not a region.** `interior detail only` is what stops a detail sub-reference
  from also asserting a cabin, a lighting state and a time of day it happens to have been cropped from.
  A sub-reference (§8.3b) needs this more than its parent does, because it was cropped out of a
  full frame and carries all of that frame's circumstances in its pixels.

Keep the definitions block **identical across every shot in a film** — it is a header, not a
per-shot field. When it changes, it changes for the whole sequence and the change is a version, so
`shot-handoff.md` §7b treats a mid-sequence definitions edit as a continuity break to reconcile,
not a local fix.

**8.3d ⚡ Wardrobe is designed for the action it must survive, not for the frame it debuts in.**
The course designs one piece of a character's look **as a removable prop** at the sheet stage —
explicitly fake, explicitly separable — because a scene forty shots later takes it off. Had it been
designed as part of the face, the removal scene would have been an edit against a locked identity: the
most expensive kind of shot to attempt and the one most likely to return a different face.

> **Ask of every costume and prop element at sheet time: does anything in the script CHANGE this?**
> Removed, torn, soaked, swapped, burned, dirtied. If yes, the sheet builds the change into the
> design — a separable piece, a two-state prop, a garment whose under-layer is specified — and
> `shot-handoff.md` §7b's `state: after` reference is generated **alongside the sheet**, not later.
> A state change that was anticipated costs a second sheet panel. One that was not costs a
> re-approval of the character.

This is the design-time half of the §7.1 state-change row in `cinematography-craft.md`, which
handles it at prompt time. Both are needed: the prompt-time rule stops you from *describing* a
transformation the reference cannot support; this one stops you from *building* a reference that
cannot support it.

**8.3e Casting is a continuity decision, not a taste decision.**
The corpus rejects a first character version because it signals the wrong story — not because it
looked worse. The sheet stage is where a character's read is set for every
shot that follows, and a read that fights the story is a note you will receive at the end of the
edit, when it costs the whole cast.

Screen sheet candidates in this order — the first two are disqualifying, the third is the choice:

1. **Light** (§8.3b) — shadow side, hair glare, underexposure. Fails here, discard, no debate.
2. **Signal** — does this person read as the role the script needs, to an audience who has not read
   the script? Wrong signal is a reject even when the face is the best in the batch.
3. **Likeness** — among what survives 1 and 2, pick the face you want.

⚠️ The owner's standing casting constraints live in memory per engagement and override step 3 — and,
where they concern read rather than looks, step 2 (e.g. for the pitcher in a sports brand's film: athletic build, not
heavy or belly-forward; no angry or stressed faces). Check them **before** generating the batch, not
while screening it.

**8.3f Several views of one character as separate references — the Seedance 2.5 route, and where it
meets §8.3b.** `[VENDOR · Higgsfield Seedance 2.5 "New capabilities (Part 2)" deck p.7–12, supplied
2026-10-25 — not measured here]` A model that takes many image references (Seedance 2.5: up to 30)
can take a character as **one image per view** instead of one sheet:
- front, back, a neutral face, and a face in a strong emotion (for the teeth and the facial dynamics);
- each declared as *"the same Mika"*, closed with *"All four images define one Mika. The output must
  contain only one such person throughout"*;
- one slot per view, so past roughly five characters the deck goes back to a sheet plus a subject
  profile (`multishot.md` §4).

How it meets the house sheet:
- ⛔ **Two face images are two candidate identities**, which is the §8.3b failure. A real photographed
  person gives faces that agree, so the route is safe there. **Generated** face views each pass the
  §8.3e screen **against the approved headshot** before they are bound; one that disagrees is dropped,
  not averaged.
- The body views stay **headless** when they are generated, exactly as the sheet's body panels are.
- The emotion view can leak its expression into neutral stages. The first job that uses it checks
  that, and checks that identity holds across the stages (`multishot.md` §7).
- The deck contradicts itself on grids (p.7 says never combine angles into one image; the p.12 example
  binds *"the three views in @Image3"*). Until a job settles it, a sheet is one reference and views are
  separate references; never both for one character in one call (a sheet's headshot plus a face view
  is two faces, §8.3b).
- Generated views are allowed only as identity locks approved at the frame gate (§8.7b).

**8.3g ⚡ The sheets a scene clip is built from (v2.49).** A scene-form clip (`prompting-craft.md` §12) has no
start frame, so the sheets are the approval point and every one is approved with its media id before any clip
binds it: `gate.py approve character:<NAME>`, `prop:<NAME>`, `world:<LOCATION>` (`ai-generation.md`, the
reference route). The gate refuses a scene clip that binds an image no approval names. The evidence for the
rules below is the festival study `[SECONDARY · festival study 2026-11-05]` and one real job's retrospective
`[STUDIO · production lessons, 2026-11-05]`.

- **One sheet per character state (HFF-06).** A character whose wardrobe changes, who is injured, soaked or
  aged, gets a **separate sheet for each state**, approved on its own (`character:<NAME>-<state>`, e.g.
  `character:MAYA-rain`). One festival team did this for every wardrobe and injury state. It is §8.3d's rule
  carried to the end: the state is an asset, never a sentence (`prompting-craft.md` §11d item 2). The clip's
  `[WARDROBE]` block names which state's sheet it binds.
- **Location sheets on a neutral base (HFF-06).** Shoot the location sheet in flat, even, neutral light, so each
  shot's `[LIGHTING]` block can relight it. A location sheet that already carries a sunset brings the sunset into
  every shot that binds it. The same team made all its location sheets this way. A real photograph still wins for
  a real place (§8.7).
- **`prop:` approvals (new stage).** A hero prop's sheet is approved like a character sheet:
  `gate.py approve prop:<NAME> --by … --quote … --artifact <sheet> --media-id <id>`. §8.5 still holds: build the
  hero prop's reference before the character's, and composite any logo or label that must be exact.
- **Look-alike characters, one colour each (HFF-05).** When two characters could be mistaken for each other, give
  each one dominant colour, in the sheet and in every prompt, **named identically every time** — *"the rust-red
  rabbit"*, never *"the red one"* in one clip and *"the ginger one"* in the next. One festival team told several
  similar rabbits apart this way. The colour goes in the cast sheet (§8.9), in `[ACTIVE REFERENCES]`, `[COLOR]` and
  `[POSITIVE LOCKS]`.
- **A wardrobe and props sheet per scene, before animating.** One sheet per scene that shows, for every character
  in it, the state of each garment and each prop: what they wear, what they hold, what sits on the table and where.
  It is made and approved before the scene's first clip, and every clip of the scene is checked against it on the
  scene board (`review-and-approval.md` §11). On a real job two shots came back with beige trousers; they were
  "fixed" to jeans to match a neighbouring shot while four other shots were beige, and a table moved between two
  shots. Nothing like this sheet existed. Manual until a script checks it.
- **Fix toward the sheet, never toward the neighbour.** When two shots disagree, the sheet decides which one is
  wrong. A fix that matches the shot next to it can spread the error to the whole scene, as the trousers did.
- **A wardrobe edit sends one image, described in words.** The source picture goes in; the new garment is written
  in `[CHANGE]` (`prompting-craft.md` §11g). A second image as a reference for the garment re-staged the whole room
  on a real job: the model read it as a second scene.
- **Confirm which model actually ran.** On the same job the service ran some Pro image edits on `nano_banana_2`.
  Read the model on the returned job before approving a sheet, and record it beside the sheet's recipe (§8.2).

**8.4 What fails, concretely.** `[SECONDARY · aifreeapi.com on Sora 2 consistency · fetched
2026-08-28 · third-party guide]` **drift begins after ~20 seconds even with an enrolled identity**;
**1–2 characters are reliable, a third causes feature blending**; **without explicit re-statement,
*"outfit colors shift, accessories appear/disappear"***; single references struggle with *"extreme
head turns."* `[UNVERIFIED]` ⛔ that article's figures (95%+ / 85–90% / 70–80%) carry **no stated
methodology — do not cite them.** `[PRIMARY · Kling AI's own blog on video drift · fetched
2026-08-28]` is a vendor documenting its own failure modes. **What drifts:** identity (*"eyes might
suddenly change color"*), wardrobe (*"a jacket suddenly changes style"*), **architecture and
dressing** (*"a bookshelf might start 'flowing' like water, or a solid wall could suddenly sprout an
extra window"*), lighting and texture. **Why:** **statelessness** — tools *"view a video as a
sequence of independent creative tasks rather than a continuous reality"*; **error accumulation**,
the *"copy of a copy problem"*; **duration**; and **no 3D awareness** — models *"can't distinguish
between a camera moving around a stationary car and the car itself changing shape."*

> **Everything §5 teaches about layering, depth, angle coverage and figure/ground exists because a
> set is a stable three-dimensional object a camera moves through. A generator has no such object —
> it has a picture and a description.** So **angle coverage is the most fragile thing you own**:
> each new angle is a new *invention*, not a new *view*. **Freeze the environment as an image and
> re-use it** — a saved Element, a tagged Reference, or Kling's *"Master Image Strategy"* of feeding
> the same image into the reference slot every time. And **background dressing drifts most and
> matters least, but the drift is visible**: specify it as **value and density** — *"dark cluttered
> shelving, low contrast"* beats *"a bookshelf with 40 books and a blue vase"*, which is a promise
> the model breaks differently in every clip. **Under-specify the background, over-specify the value
> relationship.**

**8.5 A designed prop can be held across clips — build it first.** `[PRIMARY · Google]` references
are *"of a character, object, or scene"*; `[PRIMARY · Higgsfield Elements]` makes **`prop` one of
three element categories**. `[SECONDARY · CometAPI on Midjourney V7 Omni-Reference · fetched
2026-08-28]` `--oref` covers faces, vehicles and props alike, with `--ow` **1–1000, default 100** —
25–50 for style, 100–300 balanced, **400–1000 *"essential when replicating intricate details like a
corporate logo"*** — but *"weights above 400 can yield unpredictable artifacts"*, only **one
reference image** is allowed, and it **doubles GPU cost**.

> **For a product ad, build the hero prop's persistent reference before the character's.** It is the
> shot the ad exists to deliver, and product distortion is the least forgivable failure on the reel.
> ⛔ **Where a logo or label must be exact, composite it — do not generate it.** Midjourney's own
> pairing of "high weight is essential" with "above 400 yields artifacts" describes a window both
> narrow and unreliable.

**8.6 What the discipline gives you that a prompt cannot.** **Subtraction** — `[PRIMARY · Crank]`
*"Constraint is what I think can actually help your work"*, but generators are additive: every
adjective adds an object, and the only lever is a negative tail that must be long, explicit and
repeated. This breaks most often. **History** — a generator produces the *present tense* of a room,
so accumulated history must be named as concrete residue or it will not appear. **The
lived-in/organised contradiction** — a designer holds "accumulated, mismatched, worn" and
"uncluttered where the lens goes" at once; a prompt returns the sterile catalogue room or the
undifferentiated clutter room. And a prompt is one instruction the next does not inherit, so **the
bible is the agenda**.

**8.7 Where a real photograph beats any amount of description.** The reference path exists *because*
description does not converge. **Photograph wins decisively** for any **specific identity** — a
face, a product, a logo, a real location, none of which description can converge on out of a
category — and for **texture and material** (exactly what adjectives lose), **accumulated history**
(one photo of a real room carries thousands of consistent micro-decisions no prompt can enumerate),
**colour relationships**, **lighting quality**, and **proportion** (a turnaround pins heads-tall and
eye-to-head as *measurements*). **Description wins** for **action and change**, **negation** (an
image cannot say "and nothing else"), **framing**, and **novel combinations** the reference lacks.
**The rule: reference the nouns, prompt the verbs** — every persistent noun is a saved image token,
and the prompt carries only the verbs, the framing and the negative tail (`ai-generation.md`).

**8.7b What may be a reference — the house rule (2026-10-23).** Asked how to read their standing instruction never to use AI images as references, the owner chose: *"Mood/style refs only"*. So:
- **A look, style or mood reference is a real photograph or real footage, never a generated image.** A generated image used to set the look teaches the model another model's look.
- **Allowed as identity locks, once approved at the frame gate:** the generated character, location and prop sheets of §8.3–§8.3b. They fix who and what is in the shot, not how the shot looks. (v2.49: for a scene-form clip the approval is the sheet's own stage — `character:`, `prop:` or `world:`, with its media id — §8.3g.)
- **Not AI images at all, so allowed:** grey-shape Blender blocking renders and maps drawn by code from a blocking spec. They are computer graphics built from coordinates, and they carry positions, camera and timing only.

**8.9 The bible — minimum viable art department.** Written **before** any generation, beside the
client profile (`clients/_TEMPLATE.md` §6): a **cast sheet** per character (turnaround reference;
heads-tall; eye-to-head ratio; primary shape; the *one* story function; the verbatim head-to-toe
wardrobe paragraph, "no bag" stated explicitly if none; 3-colour palette; **three personal items**;
the element ID); a **wardrobe breakdown** per character per beat (costume, state, what changed, and
that beat's reference image — the photos are the enforcement); a **location sheet** (a real
photograph preferred over a generated one; palette; **the value behind the head**; three history
objects with a backstory; background as density and value); a **props breakdown** per prop
(category; reference image; **state timeline**; whether it will be composited); a **colour script**
of 6–10 swatches in beat order; and **one reusable series-wide negative tail**.

**Three additions from the compositing side (§8.10).** Beside every saved reference, its
**recipe** — prompt, seed, model, version, settings. A **capture list** written from the script
*before* the first generation, in the pre-production sense: every persistent noun that will need a
reference, listed while making one is still free. And per composited object, its **occlusion
plan** — what will sit in front of it — and the **value and hue its background region must hold**.

**8.10 Designing so the element can be integrated.** Compositing is the craft of making a foreign
element sit in a plate, and it divides cleanly in two. The **physics of the match** — grain, black
levels, edge treatment, light wrap, contact shadow — belongs to `photorealism-physics.md` and its
remedy table, and is not restated here. The **art direction that makes the match possible at all**
belongs to this file, is decided before anything is generated, and cannot be supplied afterwards by
any amount of post.

**The acceptance criterion is stated better in VFX than anywhere above.** `[PRIMARY · Jin Zhi]`:
*"high-quality visual effects should be convincing enough for the audience to believe that all
elements in the scene were present during filming rather than added in post-production."* The test
that follows is one question — **would a viewer believe a camera filmed this?** — and it is
emphatically not *is this a good image*. Every §8 audit reduces to it.

**The decisive design move is occlusion.** `[PRIMARY · Wright]`'s **layer integration**: put the
foreground **behind** a background element so it sits *"in the plate, not on it."* That is not a
post technique — it is a dressing and blocking decision taken before generation. **Design one object
between the camera and where the composited element will go**: a shoulder, a doorframe, a leaf, a
foreground edge. An element with something in front of it reads as inhabiting the space; an element
with nothing in front of it reads as a sticker, and no amount of edge work rescues it. ⛔ For the
product close-up this is the highest-value single decision in §6, and it costs one prop.

**Design the region the element will occupy.** `[PRIMARY · Wright]`'s on-set rules are written about
a bluescreen, but the transferable core is that **isolation quality is settled by what is behind and
around the element, before capture**: uniformity first, colour purity second, and the backing colour
chosen **against the foreground's own colours** — a blue subject goes on green and the reverse. The
translation is exact: **wherever a logo, pack shot or generated prop will be composited, design that
area as uniform in value and free of the element's own hue.** A busy, contrasty, same-hue background
behind the product is a self-inflicted matte problem — free to avoid at design time, expensive at
every later point.

**Some edges cannot be isolated, and every one of them is a design choice.** `[PRIMARY · Jin Zhi]`:
there are exactly three ways to obtain a matte — **rotoscope, rendered alpha, or key** — the shot
forces the choice, and keyability is decided **before** an artist touches it, by capture quality.
`[PRIMARY · Wright]` names what defeats a keyer and needs a **holdout matte** — chrome, reflective
panels, glass, confusing colours — and the edges needing a separate soft matte: hair and motion
blur. ⛔ **A hero prop whose silhouette is chrome, glass or fringe has been designed to be
un-compositable.** Give it a matte body and a defined edge, and put the reflective material *inside*
the outline rather than on it (T39).

**Despill has a cost, and it lands on the costume.** `[PRIMARY · Wright]`: removing backing spill is
blunt and shifts real colours — his example is a naturally blue-ish red sweater losing some of its
actual blue, an artifact judged only in the finished composite, never on the isolated plate. The
design consequence: **do not dress a character, or design a prop, in a hue adjacent to whatever must
be removed or replaced behind them.** If the background will be swapped, the wardrobe token and the
background hue are one decision, not two.

**The capture list is written before the shoot, and the data cannot be recovered later.** `[PRIMARY
· Jin Zhi]` is the strongest external support in this file for §8.1 and §8.9. Modern VFX has moved
*"far beyond post"*: the shot script is read in **pre-production** to derive a capture list —
references, HDRIs, textures, survey, lens grids — and the entire discipline exists because **those
measurements cannot be recovered afterwards**; the verification is that a per-scene capture plan
exists *before* filming and is executed on set. His plain statement of the cost — *"without
reference images, visual effects artists often struggle to envision the scene when relying solely on
live-action footage"* — is this file's sentence with "footage" swapped for "prompt". **Derive the
reference list from the script before the first generation. A register you did not build cannot be
built retroactively, because the frames it would have held were never made.**

> **The tension in citing this book at all, kept.** `[PRIMARY · Jin Zhi]`'s stated position is that
> AI is a useful accelerator for tedious technical work — roto, clean plates, depth, rigging — but
> that over-reliance *"weakens the ability to think and act critically"*, and that undisclosed
> AI-generated content in a demo reel is a hiring red flag. This skill is the thing he is warning
> about. **The warning is not answered by disagreeing with it; it is answered by §8.9.** The
> practitioner who rebuilds the register — bible, breakdowns, state timelines, colour script — is
> doing the discipline he says the tools let people skip; the one who prompts and ships is the case
> he is describing. `[PRIMARY · Leandro]` names the same failure from the director's chair: the
> **DINO**, *director in name only*, who hands the sequence to the technical vendor and says "make
> that." ⛔ **Handing a brief to a model is the same abdication as handing it to a VFX house.**

**Tests. T40 occlusion** (name the object that sits between the camera and the composited element —
none means the element is a sticker) · **T41 slam** (`[PRIMARY · Wright]`'s **gamma slam**: push
gamma to roughly 3.0 and down to 0.2 on the finished frame and watch for the composited element
pulling away from the plate, or for new clipped pixels; the client's screen will do this to you
eventually, so do it first).

## 9. The gate

**A gate must emit evidence that could not exist without the work having been done.** "I checked the
silhouette" is a recollection; a black-fill PNG filed beside the frame is evidence. Run T1–T9 at
character-design approval, T14–T17 at costume approval, T18–T22 at set approval, T23–T27 at props
approval, T28–T30 at series approval, and **T10–T13 on every delivered frame**. File the artefacts
with the QA card (`realism-qa.md` §6). **If you run only four**, run these, in order of catch rate
per unit effort: **T1 (fill), T10 (thumbnail), T18 (value-behind-head), T7 (heads-tall).**

**The 2026-08-28 additions attach to the same gates.** T31–T33 run at character-design approval
**and again on every delivered key frame**, since all three are readable on a finished frame; T34
joins the costume gate; T35–T37 the set gate; T38–T39 the props gate; and **T40–T41 run on any frame
containing a composited element** — a logo, a pack shot, a real product asset — where they replace
guesswork about whether the join will survive someone else's screen.
