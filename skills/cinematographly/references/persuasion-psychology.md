# Persuasion Psychology — attention, awareness, stickiness, sound, and the ethics line

Why a video works is not a mystery to be guessed at per brief. Attention, memory, persuasion and
music have measured mechanisms, and short-form video is a small enough form that getting three of
them right is most of the job.

This file is the *why* under the craft. `craft-cheatsheet.md` says open wide and cut on action;
this says what the viewer's attention is actually doing while you do it, and how to choose a hook
from the audience rather than from the agent's taste. (v2.65.0) The person's own taste, recorded in the taste
session (`ideation.md` §0), still sets the tone and the refusals: the audience picks the hook's TYPE, and their
"never" and "cringe" answers rule out executions. **Hook evidence specifically — what is measured, what is
a vendor's house style, and what is folklore — lives in `hook-craft.md`**, which corrects two
claims on this page; they are marked where they occur.

Self-contained. Sources named inline for provenance (Styles, *The Psychology of Attention*;
Masterson & Forde, *Great Leads*; Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*; Tan, Pfordresher & Harré,
*Psychology of Music*; Hormozi, *$100M Offers*; Haugtvedt et al., *Online Consumer Psychology*;
Wänke, *Social Psychology of Consumer Behavior*) — you do not need any of them open to use this file.

## 1. Attention — what the first seconds are really fighting

(Styles, *The Psychology of Attention*)

- **Selection depends on load, not on a fixed gate** (Lavie's perceptual load theory). Under *high*
  perceptual load, irrelevant material is filtered out early; with *spare capacity*, it gets
  processed anyway. The practical read for a feed: a busy, cluttered opening frame consumes the
  load and leaves nothing for your message; **a visually simple opening frame leaves capacity for
  the words to land.** This is the mechanism behind "one thing to look at per shot."
- **Early selection keys on physical features, not meaning** (Broadbent's filter; Treisman's attenuation). In the first moments the
  viewer has not read your text — they have registered contrast, motion, colour and position. So the
  hook has to be *perceptually* distinct before it is semantically clever. A witty line on a
  low-contrast frame loses to a dull line on a striking one.
- **Attention has a refractory cost** (the attentional blink). Two demanding events in close
  succession — a text card and a spoken hook and a cut, all inside a second — and the second one is
  simply not perceived. **Stagger the load: one demand at a time.** This is the real reason a 3-second
  text card fails; it is not just reading speed, it is that the card is competing with everything
  else you put in those three seconds.
- **The spotlight has a zoom** (Posner cueing; Eriksen's zoom-lens model). A wide establishing frame spreads attention thin; a close
  frame concentrates it. Match the framing to how much precision the beat needs.

**Consequence:** the hook window is about six seconds because that is how long it takes to deliver
*one* perceptual event, *one* semantic event, and let them settle — not because six is a magic
number. **The six itself is a platform's prescription, not a measurement**
[PRIMARY · TikTok Creative Best Practices · fetched 2026-09-07 — vendor guidance, no method
disclosed]; what is above is the mechanism that makes a number in that neighbourhood plausible.
Keep them apart, and ⛔ never cite the "3-second rule" — it is Facebook's billing threshold, not a
finding. `hook-craft.md` §3 has the provenance for both.

## 2. Awareness — the question that picks the hook

The most useful pre-writing question is not "what are we selling?" but **"what does this viewer
already know?"** (Masterson & Forde, *Great Leads*).

**The five levels** (Schwartz's awareness ladder, as operationalised in Masterson & Forde, *Great Leads*) and the opening job each one needs:

| Level | What they know | The opener's job | Hook types that fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Most aware** | The product; they want the deal | Close | Offer, urgent CTA, comparison |
| **Product aware** | The product; unsure it's right for them | Reassure | Skeptic, comparison, testimonial |
| **Solution aware** | The result they want, not your product | Educate | Three things, mistake/correction, unboxing |
| **Problem aware** | The problem; not that a solution exists | Empathize | POV, problem–agitate–solution, day-in-the-life |
| **Completely unaware** | Nothing but their own life | Arrest attention | Story, curiosity gap, humour/pattern interrupt |

**The routing rule is the single highest-leverage idea in this file.** More aware → more
direct. Less aware or more jaded → more indirect. Firing a hard offer at an unaware audience chases
them off; opening a long story to a most-aware buyer who just wants the price loses them.

**This maps straight onto the hook taxonomy** in `modes/ugc-volume.md` §5 — and it is what makes a
hook bank a *system* rather than a list. A hook that underperforms is often the right hook aimed at
the wrong awareness level, not a bad hook.

**Two supporting rules:**
- **The Rule of One:** one big idea per piece. A video arguing two things persuades of neither.
- **Specificity is instant credibility:** "£312 a year" outperforms "hundreds"; "the third
  night" outperforms "quickly". This is also the safest way to make a claim strong — a specific,
  substantiated number is both more persuasive and more defensible than a vague superlative. See
  `compliance.md`.
- **Answer "what's the catch?"** (the reason-why): an unexplained good offer reads as a trick.
  Naming *why* it is possible converts suspicion into trust — and it is exactly what a skeptic-hook
  script is structurally for.

## 3. Stickiness — will they remember it tomorrow?

(Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*)

- **SUCCESs:** Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Story. **The working bar is
  three of six.** A script hitting fewer than two rarely survives testing.
- **The curse of knowledge** is the default failure of anyone who has read the brief. You know
  the product; the viewer has three seconds and no context. Test: can someone outside the project
  restate the core after one watch, without jargon?
- **Find the core, lead with it.** One sentence the whole video proves. Then the
  inverted pyramid — the core *first*, not saved for a reveal. Short-form has no second chance.
- **The curiosity gap has to be opened before it can be filled** (Loewenstein's gap theory). Curiosity is the feeling of
  a gap in knowledge — so the hook's job is to make the viewer aware they don't know something. Not
  to tease that you know something, which reads as smug. **A gap needs three legs, not one**: the
  viewer must know they don't know (salience), care about the answer (importance), and believe
  watching will fill it (epiphany). Missing any one and it is decoration. ⛔ And **more gap is not
  more better** — the concreteness/click relationship is curvilinear and turns over, so "make it
  more mysterious" is a direction, not a rule. Both findings, sourced, in `hook-craft.md` §1–§2;
  they are the operational half of this bullet.
- **Surprise must be postdictable:** the twist should feel inevitable in hindsight. A random
  surprise buys attention and spends trust.
- **Concreteness is the velcro.** Objects, actions, numbers. In video this is free — you have
  pictures — which is why "image-first, dialogue-sparse" is a memory rule and not just a craft one.
- **The Sinatra test:** one credible, specific proof beats a stack of vague ones. One detailed
  case with a measurable outcome outperforms aggregate testimonials — and, usefully, is far easier to
  substantiate.
- **Identifiable individual over statistic.** One named person moves people; a population
  number does not. Same reason personas beat demographics.

## 4. Sound — music is doing more than you think

(Tan, Pfordresher & Harré, *Psychology of Music*)

- **Music changes what the picture means.** The same footage scored differently changes
  viewers' judgments of a character's intentions, the relationship on screen, and the likely
  outcome. The viewer builds a "working narrative" from image and music jointly — so the bed is not
  decoration, it is an argument. **This is why the track is the user's call, never an autonomous
  pick.**
- **Empathetic vs anempathetic is a real choice.** Music congruent with the scene reinforces;
  music indifferent to it can intensify. Autopilot mood-matching leaves the second option unused.
  But note the trade-off: **mood-congruent audio and visual are remembered better**, so when recall
  matters (an offer, a deadline), match; when unease is the goal, don't.
- **The dial settings for expressed emotion are known** (Hevner's feature–emotion mappings, replicated across trained and untrained listeners): major mode = happy/playful,
  minor = sad/serious; fast tempo and higher loudness = arousal; consonant/simple = serene,
  dissonant/complex = agitated. **Tempo is the most cross-culturally portable cue** — for an
  international or young audience, lead with tempo, not mode.
- **~100 bpm is the body's default pulse** (Fraisse's ≈600 ms spontaneous tempo). A bed near it feels
  natural to move to. Beats are only heard as rhythm between roughly 200–1000 ms inter-onset —
  outside that window a track feels frantic or sluggish rather than driving. Light syncopation pulls
  attention *to* the downbeat, which is where you want the cut or the reveal.
- **Background music shifts behaviour below awareness:** genre cues transfer connotations onto
  the product, and tempo changes perceived value and dwell time. There is no neutral bed. Choosing
  none is also a choice — and for UGC, often the right one under the hook, because silence reads as
  unproduced.

## 5. Offer construction — what you are actually asking them to believe

A video can only carry an offer; it cannot rescue a bad one. When a script keeps failing and the
craft is fine, the problem is usually upstream. Hormozi's **Value Equation** (*$100M Offers*) is the
most useful diagnostic:

```
                Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
    Value  =  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
              Perceived Time Delay  ×  Perceived Effort & Sacrifice
```

Four drivers, and **video is unusually good at three of them:**

1. **Dream outcome** — the gap between now and the envisioned future. You don't create the desire,
   you redirect it. Note that when two competitors sell the *same* outcome, this term cancels and the
   other three do all the differentiating — which is why "we help you sleep better" is not a script.
2. **Perceived likelihood** — "will it work *for me*?" People pay for certainty. This is what proof,
   demonstration, a named case, and a guarantee buy. **Show, don't assert:** a demonstration on camera
   moves this term further than any adjective, and it is also the safest ground under
   `compliance.md`, because a shown thing is a substantiable thing.
3. **Perceived time delay** — how long before they feel it. Video moves this term cheaply and
   honestly by *showing the sequence*: what happens on day one, what week two looks like. Note the
   London Underground finding — adding a countdown display raised satisfaction more than making
   trains faster would have. **Perceived speed is a real lever, and making it visible is not a lie.**
4. **Perceived effort and sacrifice** — the work they think it costs. Again a demonstration: showing
   the thing being used in eleven seconds does more than saying "easy."

**How to use it:** when an angle underperforms, name which of the four terms the video was working
on. Most failing scripts are pushing on Dream Outcome (louder promises) when the weak term is
Likelihood (they don't believe it) or Effort (it looks like a hassle). Those need completely
different footage.

**Guarantees are a likelihood instrument, and there are several.** Unconditional ("30 days, no
questions"), conditional (money back if you followed the process), anti-guarantee (radical honesty
about who it isn't for, which reads as confidence), and implied (performance-based pricing). What
matters for a script: **a guarantee is a fact to state plainly, not a claim to dress up** — and a
guarantee about *satisfaction* is safe where a guarantee about *results* is a substantiation problem
(`compliance.md` §4).

**Scarcity and urgency — the honest forms only.** Scarcity is quantity, urgency is time.
- **Honest scarcity** is a real capacity limit, and it works because it doubles as social proof.
- **Honest urgency** is cohort-based (a real enrolment window) or seasonal (a real calendar event).
- **Pricing/bonus deadlines** are the most used and the most abused. A deadline that isn't real, or
  a timer that resets per visitor, is a hard fail on the ethics gate (§7) — and, when it is attached
  to an offer's material terms, a regulatory problem too.

The test: **could you show the viewer the mechanism behind the limit without embarrassment?** If the
scarcity is real you can explain it in one line, and explaining it makes it more persuasive, not less.

## 6. Two consumer-psychology rules worth carrying

- **Central vs peripheral route** (the Elaboration Likelihood Model; Haugtvedt et al., *Online Consumer Psychology*). High-involvement viewers process arguments; low-involvement viewers process cues — presenter
  likeability, production polish, music, social proof. **Match the video's weight to the purchase.**
  A £15 impulse product does not need an argument; a £900 considered purchase is not carried by vibes.
- **Persuasion knowledge is active** (the Persuasion Knowledge Model; Wänke, *Social Psychology of Consumer Behavior*). Viewers have a working model of how ads manipulate them, and once it fires, the tactic
  backfires. This is the deep reason "reads as AI" and "reads as an ad" hurt the same way: both trip
  the persuasion-knowledge alarm. Hedged, specific, honest copy doesn't trip it. It is also why the
  skeptic hook works — it fires the alarm *for* the viewer and defuses it.
- **Processing fluency is mistaken for truth.** Easy-to-process claims feel truer. That is a
  reason to write clearly, and a reason to be careful: fluency is not evidence, and a smooth
  unsubstantiated claim is exactly what the compliance gate exists to catch.

## 7. The ethics gate — where persuasion stops

Everything above is a lever, and levers get pulled too hard. Run this before a script ships. It is
the video counterpart of a dark-pattern review, and it is a **gate, not a guideline** — a fail goes
back to the writer, the same as a compliance fail.

A script fails the gate if it:

1. **Manufactures a fear it then sells the cure for**, especially about the viewer's body, health,
   attractiveness, or adequacy as a parent or partner.
2. **Uses false scarcity or urgency** — a deadline, a stock level, or a countdown that isn't real.
3. **Fabricates social proof** — invented ratings, review counts, endorsements, or a synthetic
   person presented as a real customer. This is also a legal line; see `compliance.md` §2.
4. **Targets a vulnerable state** — the 3am insomnia hook is legitimate; a hook engineered around
   despair, or aimed at people in a health crisis, is not.
5. **Hides the material terms** — the subscription, the auto-renewal, the true price — behind pace
   or edit.
6. **Relies on the viewer not understanding** rather than on the product being good. The test: would
   this still work if the viewer read the full offer carefully? If not, the video is doing work the
   product can't.
7. **Uses a real person's likeness or voice without rights**, or depicts a private individual synthetically.

**The honest framing:** persuasion that survives scrutiny is also the persuasion that survives
regulatory review, platform review, and a client's legal team. The ethical version and the durable
version are the same version — which is the actual business argument for running this gate at all.
